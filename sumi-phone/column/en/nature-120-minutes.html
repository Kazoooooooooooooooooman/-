Two hours a week in nature: what data on about 20,000 people showed

November 8, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

How much time do you spend in nature each week, in parks, woods or by the sea? A UK research team looked at data on about 20,000 people and found that those who spent 120 minutes or more a week in nature were more likely to say they were in good health and satisfied with life than those who spent no time there. Here are the numbers from the study, published in Scientific Reports in 2019, and what to keep in mind when reading it.

  • This was a one-time survey with self-reported answers. It cannot show that nature made people healthier

About the survey

A team led by the University of Exeter in the UK used data from MENE (the Monitor of Engagement with the Natural Environment), a UK government statistical survey, for 2014/15 and 2015/16. The data were weighted to represent the adult population.

  • What was asked: time spent in the last 7 days in nature for recreation, including parks and canals in towns, the coast and beaches, and countryside such as farmland, woodland, hills and rivers
  • Health: whether people answered "good" or "very good" to "How is your health in general?"
  • Life satisfaction: whether people scored 8 or higher out of 10 on "Overall, how satisfied are you with life nowadays?"

The comparisons accounted as far as possible for other factors, such as how green the neighborhood was, area deprivation, age, physical activity and dog ownership. Of the 19,806 people, 11,668 had spent 0 minutes in nature in the past week.

Differences started at 120 minutes a week

Splitting time in nature into 60-minute bands, the share of people who reported good health looked like this.

These shares are before adjusting for other factors. After adjusting, compared with people who spent 0 minutes, those who spent 120–179 minutes a week had 1.59 times the odds of reporting good health, and 1.23 times the odds of reporting high life satisfaction.

The researchers concluded that the positive associations peaked at around 200–300 minutes a week, with no further gain beyond that.

One long visit or several short ones: same result

It did not matter whether the 120 minutes came from one long outing or several shorter ones. Some people take long walks on weekends; others pop into a local park a little each day. The researchers suggest that people choose the kind of visit that fits their own preferences and circumstances.

The pattern held for older adults and for people with long-term illnesses or disabilities too.

The study's limits

The researchers themselves list several limits.

  • Because it is a one-time survey, they cannot rule out the reverse: healthier, happier people may simply spend more time in nature
  • Both time and health are self-reported. Self-reported time is likely to be less accurate than measuring it with location tracking
  • Time was asked about for just one randomly chosen visit in the past week, and the calculation assumed other visits were similar
  • Time in nature explained only a small part of the differences: about 1% in the models before adjusting for other factors
  • The study covers adults in England, so we do not know whether it applies directly to Japan

The researchers say that before "120 minutes a week" could be recommended as a guideline, studies that follow the same people over time and experiments that actually send people into nature are needed.

FAQ

How many minutes a week should you spend in nature?

In this study, people who spent 120 minutes or more a week were more likely to report good health and high life satisfaction, and the benefit leveled off at around 200–300 minutes a week. But since it was a one-time survey, the researchers themselves say more research is needed before it can be recommended as a guideline.

Does a local park count?

In this survey, "nature" included parks and canals in towns. And the results were the same whether the 120 minutes came from one visit or several short ones.

Summary

  • In data on 19,806 people in England, those who spent 120 minutes or more a week in nature had 1.59 times the odds of reporting good health (at 120–179 minutes a week)
  • At 1–119 minutes a week there was no difference, and the benefit leveled off at 200–300 minutes a week
  • This was a one-time, self-reported survey, so cause and effect are unclear
