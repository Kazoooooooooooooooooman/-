How to stop using your phone before bed

October 21, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

You get into bed, and before you know it you've been on your phone for an hour. The next morning, you hit snooze again and again. Experiments have confirmed that phone use before bed eats into sleep. What matters most is not blue light but two other things: your bedtime getting later, and content that keeps you scrolling. This article covers what research has found, and six things you can try starting tonight.

The short version

  • People in Japan sleep little. Around 40% of adults aged 20 and over sleep less than 6 hours a day
  • In one experiment, using a tablet before bed pushed bedtime back by about 30 minutes and shifted the body clock
  • The effect of blue light measures is unclear. What works best is keeping your phone out of the bedroom

People in Japan are not getting enough sleep

In the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's 2023 National Health and Nutrition Survey, 38.5% of men and 43.6% of women (aged 20 and over) slept less than 6 hours a day on average.

The ministry's "Sleep Guide for Health Promotion 2023" notes that adults' sleep needs vary from person to person, but recommends 6 hours or more as a guideline. Internationally, too, OECD figures (2021) put the average sleep time in Japan at 7 hours 22 minutes, the shortest of the 33 countries compared (Japan's figure comes from a 2016 survey).

What happens when you use your phone before bed

You fall asleep later and stay sleepy the next morning

In an experiment at a Harvard-affiliated hospital in the US (Chang et al., 2015), 12 healthy adults spent five nights in a row reading on an iPad for the four hours before bed, and five nights reading a printed book. On iPad nights, compared with printed-book nights, they took longer to fall asleep, their levels of the sleep-promoting hormone melatonin were suppressed, and their body clocks were delayed by more than an hour. Even after 8 hours of sleep, they were still noticeably sleepy the next morning.

Bedtime itself gets later

In another experiment by the same research group (Chinoy et al., 2018), 9 healthy adults were free to choose when to go to bed. On nights when they could use a tablet freely, their average bedtime was 10:03 p.m., compared with 9:32 p.m. on printed-book nights: about 30 minutes later.

The hour after you get into bed

In a survey of more than 45,000 university students in Norway (Hjetland et al., 2025), each extra hour of screen time after getting into bed was linked to 59% higher odds of insomnia symptoms and 24 minutes less sleep. However, this was a one-time survey that looked at associations, so it cannot show cause and effect. It made no difference whether people were on social media or something else, and the researchers think the bigger issue is not what people look at but the sleep time it eats into.

"Bedtime procrastination"

Researchers in the Netherlands (Kroese et al., 2014) gave a name to failing to go to bed at the time you planned for no particular reason: "bedtime procrastination." They found it happens more to people who usually find self-control hard, and phones feed that procrastination with endless material (why you can't stop watching short videos).

Do blue light measures work?

People often say "blue light disrupts sleep," but the research has not reached a clear conclusion yet.

  • In a statement (2024) in which the US National Sleep Foundation had 16 experts review the research, the experts agreed that for children and teens, screen use before bed and its content harm sleep. But they did not agree, for any age group, on whether the screen's light harms sleep. For adults, no conclusion was reached on any of these points
  • An analysis (2020) that pooled studies of measures such as glasses that reduce blue light before bed found small to moderate effects, with results varying from study to study

Feel free to use night mode or blue-light-blocking glasses. But if they lead you to spend longer on screens, they backfire. Change the time and the content before you worry about the light.

Six things you can try tonight

  1. Charge your phone outside the bedroom: the US CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) also lists keeping phones and other electronic devices out of the bedroom as a good sleep habit. This is the one that works best
  2. Get an alarm clock: if your phone is your alarm, it can't leave the bedroom. An alarm clock costing a few hundred yen is plenty. Without a phone by your pillow, you'll hit snooze less in the morning, too
  3. Make the hour before bed "closing time": after that time, do only things that don't involve a screen, such as getting ready for tomorrow, taking a bath or reading a printed book
  4. Have your phone's night features turn on automatically: on an iPhone, the Sleep Focus and sleep schedule, and on Android, Bedtime mode, can silence notifications and turn the screen black and white from a set time. Set them to come on by the clock rather than switching them on yourself every night
  5. Decide in advance what you'll do before sleep: "time in bed with nothing to do" becomes a reason to open your phone. Keep a printed book by your pillow
  6. Get light in the morning: open the curtains when you get up and set your body clock to morning. It helps you feel sleepy at night

Our Soto Phone has a screen that is normally black and white, with no social media or short videos. If it is just an alarm and a phone, you don't need to worry about getting sucked in even with it by your pillow. Still, whichever phone you use, the best place for it is outside the bedroom.

Summary

  • Around 40% of people in Japan sleep less than 6 hours
  • Using a tablet or phone before bed pushes back bedtime, shifts the body clock and leaves you sleepy the next morning
  • The effect of blue light measures is unclear. Change the time and the content rather than the light
  • Charge your phone outside the bedroom, get an alarm clock, and make the hour before bed closing time
