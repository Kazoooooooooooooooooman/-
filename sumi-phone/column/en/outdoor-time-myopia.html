Does time outdoors protect children from myopia? Studies from China and Taiwan, and data from Japan

November 16, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

Children who spend more time outdoors are less likely to become nearsighted. This has been tested in school-based trials, too. In primary schools in Guangzhou, China, simply adding one 40-minute outdoor class a day lowered the share of children who developed myopia over three years from 39.5% to 30.4%. Here is a summary of that trial, Taiwan's program and Japan's surveys, based on published papers and government reports.

The Guangzhou trial: 40 minutes a day outdoors

From October 2010 to October 2013, a trial at 12 primary schools in Guangzhou, China, followed 1,903 first graders (average age 6.6). The schools were split into two groups by random assignment. Six schools added one 40-minute outdoor class to every school day, and parents were encouraged to take their children outdoors after school and on weekends and holidays. The other six schools carried on as usual.

Over three years, 30.4% of children at the schools with more outdoor time became nearsighted, compared with 39.5% at the usual-routine schools, a difference of 9.1 points. The change in refraction (how far the eyes shifted toward myopia) was also smaller in the outdoor group. However, there was no clear difference in how much the eyeball grew in length (axial length).

Taiwan's Tian-Tian 120: 120 minutes outdoors every day

In September 2010, Taiwan launched a program called Tian-Tian 120 ("every day 120"), which encourages schools to take children outdoors for 120 minutes every day. Taiwan's Ministry of Education has every primary school test all students' uncorrected eyesight twice a year. Researchers analyzed these data, covering 1.2 to 1.9 million children a year, from 2001 to 2015.

The share of primary school students with reduced eyesight (uncorrected vision of about 0.8, or 20/25, or worse) rose steadily from 34.8% in 2001 to 50.0% in 2011. After the program began, it fell steadily from 49.4% in 2012 to 46.1% in 2015. Before the program, the share had been rising by 1.58 points a year; afterward, the trend turned to a fall of 2.34 points a year.

Note, though, that this study looks at a nationwide trend; it is not a randomized trial. Other factors may have played a part, and "eyesight" is not the same as myopia itself.

How bright does it need to be?

Taiwan also ran a trial with 693 first graders at 16 primary schools. Children were encouraged to go outside the classroom during breaks, raising their outdoor time to up to 11 hours a week. After one year, their eyes had shifted less toward myopia (0.35 D versus 0.47 D), and their risk of rapid myopia progression was 54% lower.

When the children wore light meters, those who spent longer outdoors at school (200 minutes or more) showed a smaller shift toward myopia. This held even when counting time at 1,000 lux or more, a fairly modest light level. The researchers concluded that strong sunlight may not be necessary, and that spending longer outdoors at lower light levels, such as in hallways or under trees, is also worth considering.

Data from Japan

In MEXT's School Health Statistics for fiscal 2025 (Japan's fiscal year runs April–March), 36.07% of elementary school students, 59.35% of junior high students and 71.51% of high school students had uncorrected eyesight below 1.0. We cover the trend in how children's eyesight is declining.

Since fiscal 2021, MEXT has also run a "Survey on Myopia among School Children" that measures myopia in detail with dedicated equipment. In the fiscal 2021 survey (29 schools nationwide, data on about 7,500 children), 20.50% of boys and 21.11% of girls in first grade had uncorrected eyesight below 1.0, rising to 57.54% of boys and 64.86% of girls in the third year of junior high (ninth grade).

In the fiscal 2022 survey (8,846 children), children who went outside more often during breaks, and who spent longer outdoors after school and on holidays, were less likely to be nearsighted. However, this analysis adjusted only for school grade, so it cannot show that time outdoors is what kept them from becoming nearsighted.

e-health net, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's health information site, says outdoor activity has been scientifically shown to prevent the onset of myopia, and that two hours of outdoor time a day is desirable.

FAQ

Does playing outside cure myopia?

What the research shows is that children become nearsighted less often, and that myopia progresses more slowly. It does not show that myopia goes away. If you are concerned, talk to an eye doctor.

Is cutting phone time enough?

Japan's survey also found that children who spend longer studying or reading at home are more likely to be nearsighted. But screen time alone does not seem to decide whether a child becomes nearsighted. We cover screens and eyes in do phones ruin your eyesight?

Summary

  • In a trial in China, a 40-minute daily outdoor class lowered 3-year myopia onset from 39.5% to 30.4%
  • In Taiwan, after a program sending children outdoors for 120 minutes a day, the share of primary school students with reduced eyesight fell
  • Strong sunshine is not required; spending longer in bright outdoor light still matters
  • Japan's health ministry site suggests two hours of outdoor time a day
