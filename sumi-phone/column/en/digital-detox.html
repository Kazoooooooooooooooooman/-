How to do a digital detox: a plan for one day, one week and one month

October 15, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

A digital detox does not mean throwing your phone away. It means spending a set amount of time away from your phone and computer. If you try to give them up for days all at once, you will usually fail. This article sums up what research has found about the benefits, and a way to build up in stages: one day, one week, then one month.

The short version

  • In one experiment, simply cutting social media down to a few tens of minutes a day reduced loneliness and low mood
  • In an experiment that switched off phones' mobile data for two weeks, 9 in 10 people improved in at least one of mood, mental health or attention
  • Start with "just one day." Once you are used to it, change your notifications and your bedroom, and finally how you carry your phone

What is a digital detox?

J-Net21, run by Japan's Organization for Small & Medium Enterprises and Regional Innovation (SMRJ), describes a digital detox as "keeping your distance from digital devices such as smartphones and computers for a certain period." It includes not only cutting them off completely, but also lighter versions, such as staying off them only while you travel.

The English term "digital detox" is fairly new: it was added to the online Oxford English Dictionary in 2013. Once smartphones spread, stepping away from them needed a name of its own.

What changes when you use your phone less

Less social media meant less loneliness and low mood

In an experiment at the University of Pennsylvania in the US (Hunt et al., 2018), 143 students were split into two groups. One group was asked to limit Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes each per day (about 30 minutes in total). Three weeks later, the students who cut back showed clearly lower loneliness and low mood than students who used social media as usual. The effect was larger for people who had been more depressed to begin with.

Quitting Facebook for four weeks freed up an hour a day

A research team from Stanford University and other institutions (Allcott et al., 2020) asked US users to stop using Facebook for four weeks. Those who stopped gained about an hour a day on average, and spent more of that time with family and friends, among other things. Their happiness rose slightly, and even after the experiment ended, they kept spending less time on Facebook.

Switching off phone internet for two weeks: 9 in 10 improved in some way

In an experiment published in 2025 (Castelo et al., PNAS Nexus), an app blocked mobile internet on 467 people's phones for two weeks. Calls and text messages still worked, and so did computers. As a result, 91% of participants improved in at least one of subjective well-being, mental health or the ability to sustain attention.

While the internet was blocked, screen time fell by about half. Only about a quarter of participants stuck with it properly for 10 or more of the 14 days, but the longer people kept it up, the more they improved.

A caution when reading the research

In every one of these experiments, participants knew they were taking part in a study about phones. The participants were also mostly people in the US. Even so, several studies agree that "cutting back even a little makes a difference."

In Japan, people feel the need but don't act on it

In a 2015 survey by MMD Labo, after the meaning of "digital detox" was explained, 2.8% of people said it was "necessary, and I do it," while 31.7% said it was "necessary, but I can't manage to do it." Even among people who know they need it, fewer than 1 in 10 actually do it. Ten years on from that survey, the time people spend on their phones has grown even further (how much time people in Japan spend on their phones).

The main reason people can't manage it is that they try to do too much at once. Start small, with the three stages below.

One day: start with half a weekend day

At first, put your phone away for just half of a day off with nothing planned (for example, from morning until noon).

  1. Decide the night before: tell your family or friends, "I won't look at my phone until noon tomorrow." If you tell them to call you for anything urgent, you can relax
  2. Charge your phone in another room: if it is within reach, your hand will keep reaching for it
  3. Decide in advance what you will do: a walk, a book, cooking, a trip to the sento (public bathhouse), and so on. If you haven't decided what to do with the free time, boredom becomes a reason to go back to your phone
  4. When it's over, write one line about how it felt: "It was easier than I expected," or "I felt most lonely around X o'clock." This gives you something to work with next time

One week: change your notifications and your bedroom

Once half a day feels fine, change just two daily habits.

Cut back notifications

In an experiment by researchers in Canada and the US (Kushlev et al., 2016), participants spent one week with notifications on and the phone close by, and another week with notifications off and the phone kept away. During the week with notifications on, they felt more inattentive and restless.

  • Turn off all notifications except calls and messages from family, school or work
  • Turn off social media and news notifications, with no exceptions

Keep your phone out of the bedroom

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "Sleep Guide 2023 for Health Promotion" also recommends not bringing phones or tablets into the bedroom and sleeping in the dark, for good sleep. Get an alarm clock, and charge your phone in the living room. The time before bed and right after waking up is the easiest time to win back.

One month: change how you carry your phone

If you keep going for a month, the key is not to put up with more, but to add more systems that mean you don't have to touch your phone.

  • Clean up your apps: delete any app you haven't opened in a month. Move the apps you open too often off the first page of your home screen
  • Hand the limits to someone else: have a family member set your Screen Time passcode (for people who keep turning off their own Screen Time limits)
  • Make days when you go out without your phone: start with walks or shopping in your neighborhood. If you need a map or a way to be reached, take a paper map or a phone that only makes calls

If it doesn't go well

If you end up looking at your phone partway through, you don't have to give up. A digital detox is not all or nothing; it is about slowly changing your distance from the phone. On days when you do look, just think back on what set it off (a notification, boredom, loneliness), and the next day, remove that trigger in advance.

That is also why we at Soto Phone are making a phone with no social media and no games: to make this "step away by design" possible every day. Go out with just calls, maps, transit and a camera, and leave your smartphone at home. We believe that is a digital detox you can keep up without having to force yourself.

Summary

  • A digital detox means keeping your distance from digital devices for a set amount of time
  • Experiments that cut back social media or switched off the internet found improvements in mood and attention
  • Start small, in this order: one day (half a day), then one week (notifications and bedroom), then one month (how you carry your phone)
  • On days when you do look, think back on the trigger, and remove it in advance next time
