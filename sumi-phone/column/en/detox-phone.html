Do you need a separate phone for a digital detox? Phone settings vs. a second phone, and how to choose

October 23, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

You do not need to buy a special phone for a digital detox. Start by trying your phone's settings for two weeks. If you still end up switching the limits off yourself, then a separate phone is worth having. Settings are "a wall you can take down"; a separate phone means the apps simply are not there. This article compares the two approaches on how well they work, what they cost and what is inconvenient, and gives a rule of thumb for deciding which one suits you.

The short version

  • The first step is your phone's settings. They cost 0 yen and you can start today
  • The weakness of settings is that you can turn them off yourself. If you turned them off again and again over two weeks, settings are not enough
  • A separate phone gives you no way to turn anything off. In exchange, you pay for the device and lose things like LINE (Japan's most-used messaging app) and mobile payments
  • If your problem is "at home, at night", use settings. If it is "the whole time I'm out", a separate phone suits you better

How the two approaches differ

Ways to cut back on phone use fall into two broad groups.

  • Set up your smartphone so it tempts you less: usage time limits, Focus modes, a grayscale screen, deleting apps and so on. You still have just one phone
  • Carry a simple phone as well: take a phone with only calls and maps when you go out, and leave the smartphone at home. This is what people call "carrying two phones"

The difference is whether you can undo it later. Settings can be switched back at any time by the person who set them. A separate phone simply does not have social media or video on it. Even if you want to undo it, you have no way to do so in your hand.

In economics, a mechanism for binding your future self is called a "commitment device", and researchers distinguish between firm "hard" ones and loose "soft" ones (Bryan et al., 2010). By that distinction, settings are a soft commitment and a separate phone is a hard one.

The most effective step is to have someone else choose the passcode for your limits. On iPhone this is the Screen Time passcode you set with "Lock Screen Time Settings"; on Android it is the PIN for app limits. Apple's iOS 26 guide says that "by default, Screen Time limits can be ignored when the limit is reached", and you need to set a passcode to stop apps from opening during Downtime. Android's Focus mode also has a "Take a break" option. Only when you hand the passcode to someone else does a limit really become a limit.

Why settings don't stick

People want tools that bind them

US economist Allcott and colleagues ran an experiment with about 2,000 US adults who use Android phones (2022, American Economic Review). When participants were given a tool that let them set their own daily time limit for each app, their use of six apps, including social media, video and the browser, fell by an average of 22 minutes a day (16%). Unlike Screen Time and similar settings, this tool was designed so that in most cases the limit could not be lifted on the spot. Even so, about half of the reduction shifted to other apps. The research team estimated that 31% of social media use comes from people being unable to stop themselves. Settings do work. But even with limits that are hard to remove, the drop was 22 minutes. For someone using their phone several hours a day, that only cuts a part of it.

Limits you can lift get lifted

In an experiment published in 2025 (Castelo et al., PNAS Nexus), 467 people in the US agreed to use an app to block the internet on their smartphone (both mobile data and Wi-Fi) for two weeks. In the group that blocked it, daily screen time fell from an average of 314 minutes to 161 minutes, roughly half. And 91% of people improved in at least one of well-being, mental health or the ability to sustain attention.

On the other hand, keeping it up was not easy. Of the 467 people who agreed to block the internet, 266 actually set up the blocking app. Only 119, about a quarter, kept it blocked for 10 or more of the 14 days. Even among people who signed up for the experiment themselves and promised to block for two weeks, most could not stick with a limit they were able to switch off. And the longer people kept it up, the more they improved.

Two things follow from this. Stepping away from your phone's internet makes a clear difference. And with a system you can switch off yourself, most people cannot keep it going.

One device holds both what you need and what tempts you

On a smartphone, messaging, maps and payments sit on the same screen as social media and video. You take out your phone to check a map, and while you are at it, a notification catches your eye. However cleverly you adjust the settings, that "while I'm at it" does not go away.

What changes when you carry a separate phone

In the experiment above, participants could still use calls and SMS, and the internet on a computer, while their phone's internet was blocked. Even so, their mental health and ability to sustain attention improved. What you really need when you are out is less than you might think.

Carrying a separate phone changes these things:

  • There is no way to switch it off: on a phone without social media or video, "just for today" never happens
  • No daily decisions: you do not have to decide each time whether to lift the limit
  • No "while I'm at it": you can use maps or make a call without notifications or recommended posts catching your eye

The honest downsides of a separate phone

  • It costs money: on top of the device, a second number means a second line. With Japan Communications' "Gōriteki Simple 290 Plan", it is 290 yen a month for up to 1 GB of data (tax included; calls are 11 yen per 30 seconds; as of October 2026)
  • LINE may not work: some 4G feature phones cannot run it, and neither can many minimal phones from overseas (minimal phones you can use in Japan, compared)
  • Mobile payments may not work: if you often pay with QR codes, you need to prepare to switch to cards or a transit IC card
  • Overuse at home stays the same: your smartphone is still at home. A separate phone does not solve picking it up at home at night or on days off
  • You carry more: if you end up carrying both phones, there is no point. You need the habit of leaving the smartphone behind when you go out

Which to choose: a rule of thumb

Phone settings suit you if

  • You mostly overuse your phone at night or at home
  • You need social media, LINE or mobile payments every day for work or school
  • You have not yet seriously tried settings (no one else holds your passcode)

A separate phone suits you if

  • Even when someone else holds the passcode, you ask them to lift the limit
  • You lifted a limit three or more times in two weeks
  • You mostly overuse your phone while commuting or out and about
  • When you are out, calls, maps, transit and a camera are about all you need

Combining both

Using both works best. Carry the separate phone when you go out, and keep settings on the smartphone at home. When you are out, the smartphone is simply not with you; at home, the limits do their job. How to handle numbers and plans with two phones is covered in "How to carry two phones".

How to decide in two weeks, starting today

Rather than thinking it through in your head, the surest way to decide whether to buy a separate phone is to test it for two weeks.

  1. Today: pick the two apps that take up the most of your time and set usage limits on them. Have a family member or friend choose your Screen Time passcode
  2. Every day: mark your calendar on any day you lifted a limit or had someone lift it for you. Add a word about what set it off (boredom, a notification, loneliness)
  3. After two weeks: if you have three marks or fewer, carry on with settings. If you have four or more, settings are not enough. Think about a separate phone to carry when you go out

The number of marks does not measure willpower. It tells you whether a wall you can take down is enough for you, or whether you need one you cannot. How to run a digital detox for a set period is covered in "How to do a digital detox".

Summary

  • You do not need to buy a special phone for a digital detox right away. Try settings for two weeks first
  • Settings are free and available today, but you can switch them off yourself. Even in the experiment that blocked the internet with an app, only about a quarter kept it up
  • A separate phone is stronger because there is no way to switch it off. In exchange, there is the cost and the inconvenience of losing things like LINE and mobile payments
  • If the problem is at home or at night, use settings; if it is while you are out, a separate phone; if both, combine them
