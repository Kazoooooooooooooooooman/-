Can't stop picking up your phone at work? Just having it on your desk lowers your focus

October 25, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

You decided to focus, and yet you find your phone in your hand. No notification came in, but you turn on the screen anyway. The problem with phones at work is less the time you spend touching them than the way they break your focus. And research shows that even if you don't touch it, just having it there takes away a little of your brainpower.

In this article

  1. Just by being there, your phone uses up brainpower
  2. Interruptions make you faster, but more tired
  3. Five ways to deal with your phone at work
  4. Summary

The short version

  • Even if you don't touch it, a phone that is simply in view reduces your available brainpower (cognitive capacity)
  • The effect is bigger for people who depend more heavily on their phones
  • Interrupted work gets done faster, but with more stress, frustration and fatigue
  • What works best is putting your phone in another room or in your bag

Just by being there, your phone uses up brainpower

Ward and colleagues at the University of Texas ran two experiments to see how having a phone nearby affects how well the brain works (2017).

They found that even people who managed not to look at their phones had less available brainpower just because their own phone was nearby. The researchers call this "brain drain." The thinking is that the effort of trying not to pay attention to the phone itself uses up brainpower. And the effect was bigger for people who depended more heavily on their phones.

In other words, "trying hard not to touch it" isn't enough. What matters is putting it somewhere out of sight.

Interruptions make you faster, but more tired

Mark and colleagues at the University of California, Irvine, ran an experiment on what happens when work is interrupted partway through (2008).

Surprisingly, people who were interrupted finished their work in less time, with no drop in quality. In return, though, their stress, frustration, time pressure and sense of effort were all higher. They were making up for the delay from interruptions by working faster.

Checking your phone every time a notification comes in may keep the work moving, but it is a way of working that builds up fatigue.

Five ways to deal with your phone at work

  1. Put your phone in another room or in your bag: as Ward's study shows, just turning it face down on the desk isn't enough. Put it somewhere you can't reach and can't see
  2. Only allow notifications that come directly from people: turn off app suggestions and news alerts, and keep only calls and work messages (which notifications to turn off and which to keep)
  3. Set times to check your phone: for example, "once an hour, for just 5 minutes," and check everything in one go. Lining this up with the breaks in the Pomodoro Technique also works
  4. Don't open social media on your computer either: if you put your phone away but do the same thing in your computer's browser, nothing changes. Keep social media tabs closed while you work
  5. Use a separate way to stay in touch for work: the easiest setup is being able to leave your personal phone at home when you head out (how to carry two phones)

Summary

  • Even if you don't touch it, just having your phone nearby uses up brainpower
  • Interrupted work gets done faster, but with more stress and fatigue
  • Rather than relying on willpower, put it somewhere out of sight
  • Something to do today: for just the next hour, put your phone in your bag while you work
