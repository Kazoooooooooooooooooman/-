How to stop reaching for your phone while studying: 5 methods that don't rely on willpower

October 15, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

You sit down at your desk to study, and before you know it you are on your phone. However many times you decide to stop, your hand reaches for it again. This is not because your willpower is weak. It is because the phone is within reach, notifications go off, and your fingers remember how to open it. So what you need to stop is not more determination but a different distance between you and your phone.

Why do we reach for our phones while studying?

There are three main reasons.

  • Notifications call you: every time a notification goes off, your mind starts wondering "What's that?" Even if you do not look, your focus is broken the moment you start to wonder.
  • Your hand remembers: unlocking the phone and opening an app is something you repeat dozens of times a day, until your hand does it without thinking.
  • It is right there: if it is on your desk, it is one second away. The moment studying gets a little hard, it becomes the easiest escape route.

Once a task is interrupted, it is hard to get back to it. In workplace research by Gloria Mark of the University of California, Irvine, it took an average of about 23 minutes to return to an interrupted task (and in the meantime, people worked on about two other tasks on average). That is why "just a quick look" while studying takes away more time than you think.

How phone use time relates to grades

Large surveys in Japan have repeatedly found that the longer students use their phones, the lower their grades tend to be.

A survey of about 22,000 junior high students in Sendai

Professor Ryuta Kawashima of Tohoku University and colleagues analyzed the academic tests and lifestyle surveys of all students at Sendai's municipal junior high schools (fiscal 2013). The average math scores were as follows.

Students who used their phones for four hours or more had lower average scores than students who barely studied, even though they studied for two hours or more.

The 2026 National Assessment of Academic Ability

The fiscal 2026 National Assessment of Academic Ability (for sixth graders and ninth graders) asked how much time students spent on social media and videos on weekdays. The average math accuracy rate for ninth graders was 48.7% among students who watched four hours or more a day, and 69.6% among those who watched less than 30 minutes.

An important caution

Both surveys show a relationship (a correlation): the longer people use their phones, the lower their grades. They do not prove that phones cause grades to fall. Still, the fact that the gap appears even when study time is the same is reason enough to cut down on phone time.

Five methods that don't rely on willpower

None of these are about "holding back." They are about setting things up in advance so that you cannot touch the phone. The ones at the top work best.

1. Put your phone in another room

What works best is physical distance. In a US experiment published in 2017 (Ward and colleagues), students who left their phones in another room did better on memory and thinking tasks than students who had their phones on the desk. The explanation is that even when the phone is switched off or face down, just having it nearby takes up part of your mind.

Later attempts to repeat this experiment did not always get the same result, and how big the effect is remains debated. Even so, putting the phone where you cannot see or reach it is a sure way to cut off both notifications and the hand habit at once.

  • Before you start studying, leave it outside your room, for example in the living room or by the front door
  • Instead of the bottom of your bag, choose somewhere it is a hassle to go and get it
  • If you need a timer, use a kitchen timer instead of your phone

2. Turn off all notifications

When you cannot put it in another room, turn off notifications. On iPhone, use a Focus mode; on Android, use Bedtime mode or Focus mode, and notifications will not come through during the hours you choose. You can also set it so that only calls from family get through.

The key is not to set it up every time you start studying, but to make it automatic by time, such as "weekdays 7–9 p.m." The fewer decisions you have to make each time, the more likely you are to keep it up.

3. Break your time into blocks (25 minutes + 5 minutes)

"Just do 25 minutes" is easier to start than "I'm going to study for three hours today," and makes it harder to escape to your phone partway through. The Pomodoro Technique, devised by Francesco Cirillo of Italy, has you focus for 25 minutes and rest for 5, and after four rounds take a longer break.

If you look at your phone during the 5-minute break, you may not make it back. The trick is to do something other than look at a screen during breaks, such as standing up for a drink of water or looking out the window.

4. Write down "what to do" on paper before you start

You often reach for your phone at the moment you are not sure what to do next. If you write your tasks down on paper in concrete terms before you start, such as "English vocabulary nos. 50–100" or "math workbook pp. 42–45," you spend less time hesitating and stop looking for an escape route.

5. Hand it to someone else

Stopping yourself is the hardest thing of all, so give the job of stopping you to someone else.

  • Give your phone to a family member just while you study
  • Agree with a friend on "60 minutes from 9 p.m. tonight" and start at the same time
  • Put it in a lockable box that will not open until the time is up (a time-locking container)

Even if you only do this at important times, such as before exams, you will see a clear effect.

If you still end up reaching for it

There is no need to blame yourself for reaching for your phone. Phones are designed to make you want to touch them. Thousands of engineers work to increase how often you open them and how long you look. Trying to beat a tool like that alone, every night, with willpower is asking too much.

On days when it does not go well, instead of thinking "my willpower was weak," look back at where your phone was and whether notifications went off. What needs fixing is not you, but where the phone is and how it is set up.

Summary

  • Reaching for your phone while studying is not about weak willpower; it is about distance, notifications and habit
  • Surveys in Sendai and nationwide show that students who use their phones longer tend to have lower grades (but this is a correlation)
  • In order of effectiveness: put it in another room → turn off notifications → work in 25-minute blocks → write down your tasks → hand it to someone
  • On days when it does not go well, fix where the phone is and how it is set up, not yourself
