How to choose a phone if you don't need social media or games: switching to something cheaper than an iPhone

October 21, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

You don't do social media. You don't play games. You use your phone for calls, messages and maps, and that's about it. Even so, plenty of people pick an iPhone costing over 100,000 yen every time they upgrade. There's no need to pay for features you don't use. This article compares four options for choosing a phone when you don't need social media or games.

The short version

  • Even the cheapest new iPhone, the iPhone 17e, costs 124,800 yen (Apple Store, after the September 2026 price rise). If you don't use social media or games, you don't need that much performance
  • Cheap Androids start in the 30,000-yen range. But you can always reinstall social media and games
  • If what matters is that you can't install them, choose a 4G feature phone or a minimal phone. The sticking point is LINE (Japan's most-used messaging app)
  • Rethink your mobile plan along with the device. If you mostly make calls, a plan costing a few hundred yen a month is enough

First, write down what you use

Before choosing a model, write down what you used your phone for over a week. Open Screen Time (iPhone) or Digital Wellbeing (Android) to see time per app. For people who don't need social media or games, it usually comes down to some of the following:

  • Calls and SMS
  • Keeping in touch with family and friends (does that include LINE?)
  • Maps and transit directions
  • Camera
  • Music
  • Cashless payments and transit IC cards (Japan's tap-to-ride cards such as Suica)
  • Looking things up (browser)

Parents choosing a phone for their child should make the list together with them. Things a parent assumes "they won't need," such as school notices or club schedules, may be essential to the child. On the other hand, when you ask what something the child says they "need" is actually for, it sometimes turns out the computer at home will do. Talking after you've made the list makes it less likely you'll argue over the choice of phone.

Mark the items on the list that you "definitely need when you're out." Looking things up can often be done on a computer or tablet at home. If a phone can do just the marked items, it's enough.

A new iPhone

The iPhone 17e went on sale in March 2026 at 99,800 yen (256GB). Its price was then raised twice, in July and September 2026, and since September it has cost 124,800 yen (ASCII.jp). Buying from a mobile carrier can cost more than the Apple Store.

The iPhone's high performance pays off for games, video editing and running lots of apps at once. If you mainly make calls, send messages and use maps, you'll hardly use that performance.

A cheap Android

Cheap Androids from Japanese makers start in the 30,000-yen range. For example, Sharp's AQUOS wish5 cost 34,980 yen (128GB) from Sharp's direct store when it went on sale in June 2025 (ASCII.jp). Calls, LINE, maps, the camera and cashless payments all work without problems.

But even a cheap one is still a smartphone inside. You can install social media, games and video at any time. It's fine for people who "aren't using them right now," but not for people who "quit, then start again."

A 4G feature phone (garaho)

These are flip phones (in Japan, 4G flip phones are often called "garaho"). They're mainly for calls and SMS, and you can install almost no apps. Their biggest strength is that you don't have to worry about reinstalling social media or games.

Their weak points are LINE and maps. Many models can't run LINE or offer only limited features, and maps and transit can't be used the way they are on a smartphone. How to choose a model and plan is covered in can you still use a flip phone in 2026?

A minimal phone

A smartphone with a narrowed set of features and no social media, games or video. Maps and music are easier to use than on a 4G feature phone, and it's less distracting than a smartphone. Many models are made overseas, so to use one in Japan you must check that it meets Japan's radio law certification (giteki) and how it handles LINE. Differences between models are compared in minimal phones you can use in Japan, compared.

What about a used or older-model iPhone?

If you want to keep using an iPhone, another option is the previous model or a used one. It's cheaper than new, and you use it the same way as before. There are two things to watch. One is the battery: used phones may have worn batteries, so buy from a shop that shows the battery's condition. The other is updates: the older the model, the sooner its OS updates end. Once updates end, banking and payment apps may stop working. A used iPhone is still an iPhone inside, so you can install social media and games at any time.

Which to choose: decide with three questions

  1. Are you likely to reinstall social media or games?: Yes → a 4G feature phone or a minimal phone. No → next question
  2. Do you definitely need LINE?: Yes → a cheap Android or an iPhone. No → a 4G feature phone is an option too
  3. Do you use maps or transit directions when you're out?: Yes → a cheap Android or a minimal phone. No → a 4G feature phone is enough

If you're unsure, give the first question priority. Even if you buy cheaply, if your phone time doesn't go down, switching is only half worth it. Do you want to spend less money, or spend less time on your phone? If both, choose a phone you can't install things on.

Does "a cheap smartphone with restrictions" work?

Another approach is to buy a cheap Android and restrict it so social media and games can't be installed. With Family Link on Android or Screen Time on iPhone, you can restrict adding apps and how long they're used. In Japan, the law requires mobile carriers to explain and set up filtering when a child under 18 signs up for a smartphone.

Whether it works depends on who holds the passcode.

  • For a child's phone: If a parent sets and manages the passcode, it works well
  • If you're restricting yourself: If you set the passcode yourself, you'll switch it off yourself when things get hard (for people who keep switching off their own Screen Time limits)

Also, even with restrictions, if the browser works, social media and videos can be viewed through it. In the Children and Families Agency's fiscal 2025 survey, high school students used the internet for about 6 hours 44 minutes per weekday. Holding back a tool used for that long with settings alone isn't easy. If you know you can't hold back by willpower, choosing a phone you can't install things on in the first place is the surer route.

Rethink your plan along with the device

If you don't look at social media or videos, you use almost no mobile data. Just switching from a big carrier plan to a cheap one lowers your monthly bill a lot. For example, Japan Communications' "Gouriteki Simple 290" plan gives you 1GB for 290 yen a month (call charges extra). How to choose a plan for a second phone is compared in how to carry "two phones" so you can leave your smartphone at home.

Watch out for device discounts too. Since December 2023, whether you buy a device with a contract or on its own, the cap on discounts is set according to the device's price, up to a maximum of 40,000 yen (ITmedia Mobile). Deals like "effectively 1 yen" are often conditional on returning the device after, for example, two years. If you don't return it, you pay the remaining cost. Check numbers that look cheap against the total cost and the conditions.

What to check so it lasts

Even if you buy cheaply, it's expensive if it becomes unusable after two years. Before buying, check these three things:

  • How long updates last: How many years the maker says it will keep providing OS and security updates. This is often stated on the product page or in the announcement
  • Battery: Whether the battery can be replaced, and whether there is a place to get it replaced
  • Whether it works on your network: Especially for overseas models and used phones, check the maker's or carrier's list of tested devices to confirm it supports your carrier's network

What students and parents should check before switching

  • School and club communication: If everything goes through LINE, choose a phone that can use LINE, or ask to receive messages by email or phone too (how to stay in touch without LINE)
  • Getting to school: Transit IC passes can be carried as cards. If you rely only on mobile payments on your phone, get a card
  • Applications and submitting assignments: University applications and school assignments can often be done on a computer or tablet at home. You don't have to do everything on your phone
  • Verification codes: Some online services send a verification code by SMS when you log in. Keeping the same phone number saves trouble
  • Agreements with your family: If it's for a child, decide what it may be used for before you buy (what age should kids get a smartphone?)
