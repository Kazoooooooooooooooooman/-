What is WHO's "gaming disorder"? The definition, and how to read Japan's survey numbers

November 16, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

The World Health Organization (WHO) added "gaming disorder" to ICD-11, the international classification of diseases. ICD-11 came into effect on January 1, 2022. That does not mean everyone who loves games, or plays for a long time, has it. Here is WHO's definition, along with numbers from a national survey in Japan and the caveats you need to read them.

WHO's definition: three features, and harm to daily life

WHO defines gaming disorder as a pattern of gaming with the three features below. It covers digital gaming and video gaming.

FeatureWhat it means
Impaired controlYou cannot decide for yourself how long to play or when to stop
Gaming comes firstGaming takes precedence over other interests and daily activities
Carrying on despite harmYou keep playing, or play even more, even when problems arise

On top of that, the pattern must cause significant impairment in important areas of life, such as family, friends, school or work. As a guide for diagnosis, this would normally have been evident for at least 12 months. It is not decided by playing time alone.

In Japanese, the condition has usually been called "game shogai" (gaming disorder), but the term "game kodo-sho" (roughly, "gaming behavior disorder") is now also used, for example in reports by Kurihama Medical and Addiction Center.

Most people who play games are not affected

WHO says that, according to studies, gaming disorder affects "only a small proportion" of people who play digital or video games. It adds that people who play should be alert to:

  • How much time they spend gaming, especially when it pushes out other daily activities
  • Any changes in their physical or mental health or their social life that could be linked to their gaming

On why gaming disorder was included in ICD-11, WHO points to a consensus of experts from different disciplines and regions, the fact that treatment programs for people with this kind of condition already exist in many parts of the world, and the hope that health professionals will pay more attention to prevention and treatment.

How common is it in Japan?

Kurihama Medical and Addiction Center ran a national survey in fiscal 2024 (Japan's fiscal year runs April-March) as a project funded by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. Fieldwork took place in January-February 2025, and 4,650 responses from people aged 10 to 80 were analyzed. People who scored 5 or more on the nine-question "GAMES test" were counted as "suspected" of ICD-11 gaming disorder.

Overall (age-adjusted), the share was 3.8% (men 5.4%, women 2.3%). Among 10-29-year-olds it was 10.3% (men 14.6%, women 5.8%). The younger the age group, the higher the share.

Compared with a fiscal 2018 survey of 10-29-year-olds, the share rose from 5.1% to 10.3%, which the report describes as a significant increase.

Caveats when reading the numbers

  • "Suspected" is based on people's own answers to a short questionnaire. It is not the share of people diagnosed by a doctor
  • With a different questionnaire (the IGDT-10), the "suspected" share was 0.7% overall and 2.4% among 10-29-year-olds. The numbers change a lot depending on the questionnaire
  • On the IGDT-10, the rise from 1.8% in fiscal 2018 to 2.4% was not a statistically clear difference
  • In the 2018 and 2019 surveys, field staff visited homes to ask for cooperation; in fiscal 2024 the survey was sent by mail. The report says simple comparisons with earlier surveys need care
  • The valid response rate was 51.7%

A rising share is a reason to pay attention. But this data does not support saying that "one in ten young people has a gaming illness."

Playing time, and what to do if you are worried

An earlier survey by the same center found that the longer people played each day, the more likely they were to say they "could not stop when they should have" or that their sleep schedule had flipped. We covered this in data on young people's gaming time.

If you are worried about how you or a family member plays, you do not have to deal with it alone. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare points people to their local public health center or mental health and welfare center (some are called "kokoro no kenko center", or mental health center) as places to talk about addiction.

Rather than aiming only to quit gaming, another approach is to gradually add other things to enjoy, such as plans to go out or time spent with people.

FAQ

Does playing games for a long time mean you have gaming disorder?

Playing time alone does not decide it. Under WHO's definition, the features of impaired control, gaming coming first, and carrying on despite problems must come together with serious harm to daily life, normally lasting at least 12 months. WHO says it affects only a small proportion of people who play games.

How many people in Japan have gaming disorder?

There is no national count based on doctors' diagnoses. In Kurihama Medical and Addiction Center's fiscal 2024 survey, 3.8% of people aged 10-79 and 10.3% of 10-29-year-olds screened as "suspected" on a questionnaire. With a different questionnaire the figures were 0.7% overall and 2.4% for 10-29-year-olds, so the numbers depend on the questionnaire used.

Summary

  • Gaming disorder is part of ICD-11 (in effect since January 2022). It requires three features plus serious harm to daily life, normally lasting at least 12 months
  • WHO says it affects only a small proportion of people who play games
  • Japan's fiscal 2024 survey found 3.8% "suspected" overall and 10.3% among 10-29-year-olds. These are not diagnoses, and the numbers change with the questionnaire and survey method
