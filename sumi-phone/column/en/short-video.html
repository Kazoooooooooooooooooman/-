Why you can't stop watching short videos, and how to step away

October 15, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

You open a short video meaning to watch "just one", and suddenly it's been 30 minutes. If you find it hard to stop on TikTok, YouTube Shorts or Instagram Reels, it is not because your willpower is weak. They never end, the next one starts on its own, and the more you watch, the closer they match your taste. That is how they are built. This article covers the design that makes them hard to stop, what research has found, the settings in each app, and ways of stepping away that work better than settings.

The short version

  • There are three reasons they are hard to stop: they never end, the next video plays automatically, and the recommendations keep learning
  • People who watch more short videos tend to score lower on measures of attention and mood (though this is a correlation)
  • Most in-app time limits only "remind" you; they don't stop you
  • What works best is deciding where and when you watch, and making the app impossible to open the rest of the time

Why is it so hard to stop?

1. There is no end

Flick the screen up and another video appears, endlessly. There is no "break" like the end of a chapter in a book or the closing credits of a TV show. When there is a break, people think "I'll stop here". Without one, you have to come up with a reason to stop yourself.

2. The next one starts automatically

When one video ends, the next plays without you doing anything. Because it continues even if you never decide to "watch", it won't stop unless you decide to "stop".

3. The recommendations keep learning

TikTok officially explains that it decides the order of videos from many factors, such as how long you watched and whether you tapped "Not interested". The more you watch, the closer the next video gets to your taste.

4. "The next one might be good"

Among the videos that come up, some are dull and some are really entertaining. It is often explained that the hope that "the next one might be a hit" is what keeps you watching. However, research that isolates this mechanism alone and tests it on people is still limited.

In February 2026, the European Commission issued a preliminary finding that TikTok's infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and highly personalized recommendations could weaken users' self-control and breach the EU's Digital Services Act (this is not a final decision). Difficulty stopping is starting to be treated not as a personal problem but as a design problem.

What research has found

  • Links with attention and mood: a 2025 analysis pooling 71 studies (about 98,000 people) (Nguyen et al., Psychological Bulletin) found that people who use short videos more tended to score lower on measures of attention and impulse control, and higher on stress and anxiety. However, which causes which is not known
  • Forgetting "things to do later": in an experiment with 60 people (Chiossi et al., 2023), only watching TikTok lowered people's ability to remember things they had decided to "do later". Twitter, YouTube (long videos) and doing nothing did not lower it. The rapid switching from one short video to the next is thought to play a part
  • What makes people stop lies outside the app: in a one-week study of 46 people (Rixen et al., 2023), most endless-scrolling sessions ended because of something outside the app, such as someone calling them or it being time for a plan

The third finding matters when thinking about how to step away. The cue to stop needs to be set up outside the app, not inside it.

Settings in each app to help you step away

Every app has a feature that tells you when you are using it too much. However, most of them only show a reminder; they don't stop you. Set them up, then combine them with the approaches in the next section.

TikTok

  • Daily screen time: under "Settings and privacy" → "Screen time", you can set a daily limit. When you reach it, you need a passcode to keep watching
  • Break reminders: if you keep watching, a screen appears suggesting you take a break
  • Sleep reminders: at a time you choose, it tells you it's time for bed
  • For accounts of 13- to 17-year-olds, a 60-minute daily limit is on by default

YouTube

  • Shorts feed limit: under "You" → "Settings" → "Time management" → "Shorts feed limit", choose a daily limit for watching Shorts. You can even set it to 0. When you reach the limit a reminder appears, but you can also choose to ignore it and keep watching
  • Take a break reminder: "Settings" → "General" → "Remind me to take a break". After the time you set, the video pauses and suggests a break. On by default for 13- to 17-year-olds (a feature of the phone and tablet apps)
  • Bedtime reminder: "Settings" → "General" → "Remind me when it's bedtime". At the time you set, it tells you it's time for bed
  • Turn off or delete watch history: profile picture → "Settings" → "Manage all history" → in the "Controls" tab, uncheck "Include your YouTube History", and videos you watch after that will not be saved to history. YouTube says that if you don't want recommendations on your home page, one way is to delete and turn off your watch history
  • "Not interested": from the More icon next to a recommended video, choose "Not interested" or "Don't recommend channel" to see fewer of the videos and channels you don't want

YouTube's menu names can change with app updates and between devices. If you can't find one, look inside the app's "Settings".

Instagram

  • Daily time reminder: tells you when you have used the time you set (not available on the computer version)
  • Teen Accounts (ages 13–17): after 60 minutes a day, a notification tells them to close the app. From 10 p.m. to 7 a.m., "Sleep mode" silences notifications. Users under 16 need a parent's approval to loosen the settings

Three ways of stepping away that work better than settings

1. Decide where and when you watch

Decide in advance where and when watching is OK, such as "only on the train home" or "only for 20 minutes after dinner". This brings a break in from outside to an app that has none. It also fits the research finding that the cue to stop lies outside the app.

2. Take it off your home screen, or delete it

Move short-video apps off the first page of your home screen, or go all the way and delete them. Reinstalling them only on weekends is fine too. Adding just one extra step before you can open them cuts down a lot on "opening them without thinking".

3. Before bed and while studying, keep your phone in another room

The times short videos take the most from you are in bed before sleep and in the gaps while you study or work. In these two situations, put the phone itself somewhere out of reach (how to stop reaching for your phone while studying).

On days when there is something you want to watch, one option is to choose a longer video or a film that has an ending, rather than Shorts. Because "it's over when it ends", it is easier to decide your own time.

The phone we are making at Soto Phone has no short videos and no social media. Our idea is not to carry apps without a stopping point in the first place, so that time outside is time when you don't have to look for a moment to stop.

Summary

  • Short videos are hard to stop because they never end, the next one plays automatically, and the recommendations keep learning
  • People who watch more tend to score lower on measures of attention and mood (a correlation)
  • App limits are mostly reminders, so don't rely on settings alone
  • Decide where and when you watch, keep the apps at a distance, and put your phone in another room before bed and while studying
