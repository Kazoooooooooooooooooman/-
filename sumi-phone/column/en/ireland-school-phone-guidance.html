Ireland's school phone guidance: a "ban" in primary schools, a "restriction" in post-primary schools, and money for storage

November 16, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

In Ireland, students do not use their phones at school, not even at break or lunchtime. In circulars issued in June 2025, the Department of Education required every primary school to "ban" phones and every post-primary school (the equivalent of junior high and high school in Japan) to "restrict" them. It set aside €9 million for pouches and boxes to store phones, paying schools €25 per student. This summary is based on the department's circulars and Irish press reports.

It began with a minister's letter in August 2024

On August 28, 2024, then Minister for Education Norma Foley wrote to principals and boards of management across the country. The letter set out her plan for school days free of phone use, in both primary and post-primary schools. The reasons it gave were distraction in class, the risk of seeing inappropriate content and of cyberbullying, and less face-to-face time with friends.

The letter asked each school to decide how to do this after talking with students and parents. Its examples included lockers, cubbies, and keeping phones switched off in students' bags. It also said parents could still reach their children through the school office in an emergency, as before, and that students could carry their phones on the way to and from school.

The policy builds on "Keeping Childhood Smartphone Free," an initiative launched in 2023 that helps parents agree among themselves, voluntarily, not to buy smartphones for their children while they are in primary school.

The June 2025 circulars: a "ban" for primary, a "restriction" for post-primary

In June 2025, the department issued one circular for primary schools and one for post-primary schools. Both require schools to adopt a policy that keeps phones out of use for the whole school day, breaks and lunchtime included, and to publish it on the school website.

The circulars also allow exemptions: for example, students with diabetes whose glucose monitor works through a phone, students whose seizure condition needs monitoring through an app, students with special educational needs, and students learning English as an additional language who use language apps. An exemption covers only its stated purpose, not general use.

Using tablets and computers in class continues as before.

Storage funding: €25 per student

Budget 2025 set aside €9 million for phone storage in post-primary schools. For three years after 2025, up to €1 million a year more is available to replace equipment.

Schools that buy locking pouches, storage boxes or similar can apply for €25 per student. Unspent money cannot be used for anything else; it must be kept for later needs, such as equipment for the next year's first-year students.

According to press reports, as of December 2025 just under €6.5 million had been paid to 472 non-fee-charging post-primary schools. Of the schools that could apply, 190 had not. Applications closed on November 28, 2025, and a May 2026 report said €7.1 million in total had gone to 519 schools.

Criticism, and what research shows

The original plan was for the state to buy pouches centrally. A tender went out in January 2025 (reported at €7.3 million excluding VAT), but it was dropped on advice from the Chief State Solicitor's Office. The scheme was then changed so the money could also go to lockable boxes or handing phones in at the office. Opposition politicians called it a "screeching U-turn" and pointed out that many schools are short of money.

On children's phones in general, the Irish children's online safety charity CyberSafeKids surveyed about 9,000 children. According to press reports, smartphone ownership among 8- to 12-year-olds fell to 39% from 49% the year before.

Whether school phone bans work is still an open question. A 2025 study of 1,227 students aged 12 to 15 at 30 schools in England found no difference in mental wellbeing scores between schools that restrict phones and schools that allow them. Students at restrictive schools spent about 40 minutes (0.67 hours) less on their phones during the school day, but there was no difference in their phone time across weekdays or weekends as a whole. It seems that making rules at school is not enough on its own; how children spend time at home matters too.

FAQ

Are phones banned in Irish schools?

Yes. Under the Department of Education's June 2025 circulars, primary schools must ban phone use for the school day and post-primary schools must restrict it. Both include breaks and lunchtime.

What are other countries doing?

School phone bans have spread to many countries. We cover the country-by-country picture in school phone bans now cover 58% of the world's countries. For Ireland's neighbor, see England's school phone guidance.
