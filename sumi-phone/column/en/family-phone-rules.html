Family phone rules without the fights: step-by-step, with example rules

October 24, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

Parents may think "we have phone rules at home," while their children don't think so at all. In the latest survey by Japan's Children and Families Agency, parents' and children's answers in families with high school students differed by as much as 14 points. A rule your child doesn't know about is the same as no rule. This article walks through how to make rules that actually get followed: not by having parents decide and impose them, but by deciding them together with your child.

Parents say "yes," children say "no": the gap the survey found

The Children and Families Agency's "Fiscal 2025 Survey on the Internet Usage Environment of Young People" asked children who use the internet, and their parents, whether their family has rules.

  • The higher the grade, the lower the share who say they have set rules
  • In every grade, parents are more likely than children to say there are rules
  • Among high school students, 44.3% of children said so, compared with 58.3% of parents. A gap of 14 points

Parents may believe they set rules, but those rules have not reached their children as "rules." The gap grows especially large by high school.

Five steps to rules without the fights

  1. Talk with your child at a calm moment: not right after scolding them about their phone, but when you both have some breathing room, such as after a meal
  2. Hear your child's side first: "Why do you need that time?" "What would be a problem for keeping in touch with friends?" Once you understand their situation, the rules will be realistic
  3. Decide just three things: "time," "place" and "what happens if a rule is broken." The more rules you make, the harder they are to keep
  4. Parents follow the same rules: if phones stay off the dinner table, that includes yours
  5. Write them on paper and put them where everyone can see: this avoids "we agreed" versus "nobody told me." Review them together every three months

The American Academy of Pediatrics publishes a tool called the "Family Media Plan," which families use to talk through and decide how they use media. The idea is the same: each family decides for itself and writes it down.

Example rules you can use as they are

What to decideExample rule
TimeUntil 9 p.m. on weekdays. Until 8 p.m. in the week before a test
PlaceAt bedtime, the phone charges in the living room. It never goes on the dinner table
MoneyTalk to a parent before any in-app purchases or shopping
When something goes wrongIf a stranger contacts you or something upsetting happens, always tell us. You won't be scolded
If a rule is brokenThe next day, phone time ends at 8 p.m. (the phone is not taken away; the time is just shorter)

The "when something goes wrong" rule matters most. If children think they will be scolded, they hide their problems. Promise in advance that "if you tell us, you won't be scolded," and you will notice trouble sooner.

When the rules stop being followed

  • Don't take the phone away straight off: taking it away tends to push children into using it in secret. First, work out together which part wasn't kept
  • Review the rules themselves: if they can't be kept, they may be too strict. Talk about whether they fit your child's life
  • Notice the days the rules were kept: rules last longer when you speak up on the good days rather than the bad ones

Summary

  • The older the children, the more parents and children disagree on whether there are rules
  • Make rules together with your child, and decide just three things: "time," "place" and "what happens if a rule is broken"
  • Parents follow the same rules. Write them down, put them up, and review them every three months
  • Something to do today: after dinner, take 10 minutes to ask your child, "Is anything about your phone bothering you?"
