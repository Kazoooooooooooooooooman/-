What age should kids get a smartphone? Five things to decide before you hand one over

October 15, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

At what age should a child get a smartphone? There is no right answer, but there is data to guide you. In a 2025 survey, Japanese children got their first smartphone at 10.2 years old on average, and more than half had one by fifth grade. This article covers the latest surveys, how rules are changing in Japan and abroad, and five things to decide as a family before you hand over a phone.

The short version

  • The average age for a first smartphone is 10.2 (10.4 for boys, 9.9 for girls). More than half have one by fifth grade, and over 80% by the first year of junior high school (age 12–13)
  • Abroad, Australia restricts social media for under-16s by law, and in the US a movement of parents pledging to "wait until 8th grade" is growing
  • More important than age is deciding, before the phone arrives, why your child needs it, when and where it can be used, filtering, social media, and a date to review the rules

At what age do children get smartphones?

In a survey of 1,200 pairs of elementary and junior high school students and their parents across Japan, carried out in November 2025 by the NTT DOCOMO Mobile Society Research Institute, the average age at which children got their first smartphone was 10.2. For girls it was 9.9, the first time it has fallen below 10 since the survey began in 2018. More than half of children own one by fifth grade, and over 80% by the first year of junior high school.

In a survey by the Children and Families Agency (fiscal 2025, ages 10 to 17), the share of children who use a smartphone to go online rises with each school level.

In elementary school it is about half, but in junior high it jumps to over 80%. Starting junior high school is clearly a major turning point for many families.

How the rules are changing in Japan and abroad

Australia: social media for under-16s restricted by law

Since December 10, 2025, platforms in Australia have been required to stop under-16s from holding social media accounts. Ten services are covered, including TikTok, Instagram, YouTube and X, and platforms that break the rules face fines of up to A$49.5 million. There are no penalties for children or parents.

United States: parents who "wait until 8th"

"Wait Until 8th" is a US movement in which parents pledge not to give their child a smartphone until at least the end of 8th grade (the second year of junior high school in Japan). The pledge takes effect once 10 or more families in the same school and grade have signed, so no child is the only one without a phone. Basic phones for calls and texts only are allowed. More than 147,000 pledges have been signed (as of April 2026).

United Kingdom: no phones during the school day

The UK Department for Education says phones should not be used at any point while children are at school, including breaks, not just during lessons.

Japan: phones at school, and Toyoake City's ordinance

A 2020 notice from the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) says that, in principle, elementary school students should not bring phones to school. Junior high schools are also no-phone in principle, but may allow phones if the school, students and parents agree and conditions such as setting up filtering are met.

In Toyoake City, Aichi Prefecture, an ordinance took effect in October 2025 encouraging people to keep smartphone use outside work and study to "within two hours a day" as a guideline. There are no penalties. It also asks elementary school children and younger to avoid using phones after 9 p.m., and junior high students and older after 10 p.m. Its main aim is to protect sleep.

The worries about getting a phone early

  • Sleep: Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare sleep guide recommends 9 to 12 hours of sleep for elementary school children and 8 to 10 hours for junior high and high school students, and advises against using digital devices in bed
  • Mental health: In a 2023 advisory, the US Surgeon General cited research finding that 12- to 15-year-olds who use social media for more than three hours a day face double the risk of symptoms of depression and anxiety (whether social media is the cause has not yet been established)
  • School performance: Surveys in Japan have repeatedly found that children who use phones for longer tend to get lower grades (see how to stop reaching for your phone while studying)

Five things to decide before you hand over a phone

1. What the phone is for

The first thing to decide is the purpose. If the goal is "to stay in touch on the way home from juku (private cram school)" or "to know where they are," a smartphone is more than you need. A kids' phone (kids keitai) is a mobile phone limited to calls, messages and location checks; DOCOMO's "Kids Keitai Plan" costs 550 yen a month (tax included). Depending on the purpose, starting with a simpler device is one option.

2. When and where it can be used

Decide "until what time" and "where." Most important of all: no phones in the bedroom at night. Choosing one charging spot in the living room makes this easier to stick to.

3. Filtering

In the Children and Families Agency survey, 85.8% of parents whose children use a smartphone managed their child's internet use in some way, and the most common method was filtering (49.2%). The surest approach is to have the carrier's store staff set it up when you sign the contract.

4. When social media can start

Most social media services require users to be 13 or older in their terms of use. Decide separately whether your child can have a smartphone and whether they can use social media. Some families take it in steps, such as "a smartphone in sixth grade, social media from junior high."

5. A date to review the rules

The rules you set at first will not necessarily fit forever. If you agree on a review date at the start, such as "before summer vacation" or "when the school year changes," a broken rule becomes a chance to talk things over rather than just a reason to scold.

Summary

  • The average age for a first smartphone is 10.2, and ownership passes 80% when children start junior high school
  • Abroad, social media restrictions for under-16s and the "wait until 8th grade" movement are spreading
  • Before thinking about age, decide the purpose, when and where, filtering, social media, and a review date
  • If the goal is staying in touch or knowing where your child is, starting with a simpler phone is one option
