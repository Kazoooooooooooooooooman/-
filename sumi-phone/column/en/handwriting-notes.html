Do you remember more when you take notes by hand? A famous study, and the replications that followed

November 12, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

You may have heard that notes taken by hand stick better. The idea comes from a study published in 2014 in the US journal Psychological Science. But two later studies that repeated the same method found no clear difference between handwriting and a laptop. Right now, we cannot say for sure either that "handwriting wins" or that "a laptop is just as good." This summary is based on the papers' abstracts and an explainer from the Association for Psychological Science.

The short version

  • In the 2014 study, students who took notes on a laptop scored lower on conceptual questions than those who wrote by hand
  • In replications published in 2019 and 2021, the differences were small and not statistically significant
  • The replications did not find handwriting to be worse, either. For now, we cannot say which is better

What the 2014 study found

Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer in the US had university students watch lecture videos and take notes either on a laptop or by hand. Afterward, the students answered questions that tested whether they remembered facts, and questions that asked them to understand and apply the ideas.

  • In three studies, students who took notes on a laptop scored lower on conceptual questions than students who wrote by hand
  • Laptop notes contained more of the lecturer's words copied word for word
  • The researchers explained that copying word for word means less work putting ideas into your own words, so learning is shallower

The study drew attention because it compared the act of note-taking itself, rather than the familiar problem of students getting distracted by social media or the web in class. Study 1 involved 67 students at Princeton University.

When the study was repeated, the difference was unclear

In psychology, other researchers often repeat a well-known study with the same method to see whether they get the same result. This is called a replication. This study has had two replications.

The 2021 replication used about twice as many people as the original experiment. Even so, it found no clear difference between handwriting and a laptop.

Reading the replications without overstating them

This is where it is easy to get the wrong idea.

  • They do not say handwriting is pointless: in the 2019 paper, combining the replication experiments pointed slightly in favor of handwriting. But the effect was small and not statistically significant
  • They do not say laptops are better either: in the 2021 study, the laptop group did not outperform the handwriting group
  • The effect of copying word for word is unclear: the replications agreed that laptop notes contain more of the lecturer's exact words. But in the 2021 study, the link between more word-for-word copying and lower scores did not hold up reliably

The authors of the 2019 paper wrote that concluding which method is better "seems premature." The authors of the 2021 paper likewise said their results do not support the idea that handwriting notes helps you remember more on a test right afterward.

What these experiments cannot tell us yet

  • Every experiment had students watch a short lecture video and then take a test. That is different from weeks of classes or a real exam
  • In the 2021 experiment, students had no time to review their notes before the test. Results might change when students can review
  • In the 2019 study, the differences between groups shrank further after students studied their notes
  • In the experiments, the laptop was used only for taking notes. That is not the same as a laptop in an everyday class, with notifications and social media arriving

When you use a laptop in a real class, avoiding other apps and notifications may matter more than how you take notes. We collected ways to focus on laptop work in how to focus on a report, and tips on phones while studying in how to stop picking up your phone while you study.

FAQ

Do you remember more if you take notes by hand?

The 2014 study found that, but replications in 2019 and 2021 found no clear difference between handwriting and a laptop. For now, we cannot say that handwriting always helps you remember more. It is fine to use whichever you find easier.

Should you review your notes afterward?

In the 2019 study, the differences between handwriting, laptops and the other groups shrank further after students reviewed their notes. Reviewing your notes later may matter more than which tool you use to take them.

Summary

  • In the 2014 study, handwritten notes did better on conceptual questions
  • Studies in 2019 and 2021 that repeated the method found no statistically significant difference
  • We cannot yet say which is better. A distraction-free setting and reviewing your notes are worth more attention than the tool
