Does taking away the phone backfire? What parents can do

October 27, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

Taking away a child's phone sometimes works, and sometimes backfires. Looking at the research, what makes the difference is less "whether you take it away" and more the child's age and how you set limits. This article covers what research has found, what to try before taking the phone away, and how to do it if you decide to.

The short version

  • A paper reviewing 27 studies from outside Japan could not say for certain whether restricting phone use makes things better or worse
  • What was clear: doing nothing tends to make things worse, and talking things through tends to help. The closer the family, the less overuse there was
  • Limits tend to work up to around primary school age. The older the child gets toward high school, the better it works to let them decide for themselves

What the research shows

A review published in 2019 compared 27 studies (about 94,000 people in total) on how parents' involvement relates to teens' overuse of the internet and online games. It sorts parental involvement into four types.

ApproachWhat it meansLink to overuse
Doing nothingLeaving it entirely to the childTends to make things worse
Using it togetherWatching videos or playing games togetherUnclear
Talking it throughTalking about how they use it and what they seeInternet overuse tends to drop. Unclear for games
RestrictingSetting time limits, taking the phone away, turning off featuresBetter in some studies, worse in others. Depends on how limits are set

The same paper also found less overuse in families with strong cohesion, and more in families with frequent conflict. The authors note that all of these findings come from surveys, and that higher-quality research is needed to confirm them.

What works changes with age

A 2025 study of 1,673 young people aged 10 to 18 in Taiwan found differences by age.

  • Ages 10 to 12: the more closely parents watched over and set limits, the less overuse there was
  • As children got older: limits had less effect
  • Ages 16 to 18: approaches that supported them in thinking things through and deciding for themselves were a better fit
  • At every age: vague watching, without knowing what the child was actually looking at, did not work

This study is also survey-based, so it cannot show that limits caused the drop in overuse. Still, the idea that "parents decide while children are young, and children decide more as they grow" is consistent with other research.

In Japanese families, parents and children see the rules differently

According to an article reporting on a fiscal 2024 survey (preliminary results) by the Children and Families Agency, 61.1% of parents of high school students said "we have rules", compared with 46.6% of the high school students themselves. That is a gap of about 14.5 percentage points, and it widens as children move up through school.

Parents may think they have set rules while their children don't think there are any. Taking the phone away suddenly in that situation easily looks to the child like a punishment with no reason.

What to try before taking it away

  1. Ask what's bothering them: before scolding, ask "Is anything about your phone bothering you lately?" Often the child already feels they want to stop but can't
  2. Set the rules together: decide just three things with your child, "when", "where" and "what happens if the rule is broken", and write them on paper. We explain how in how to make family phone rules without fights
  3. Decide where the phone lives: for example, charge it outside the bedroom at night, and keep it off the dinner table. "Putting it in its place" is easier to accept than having it taken away
  4. Parents follow the same rules: if parents look at their phones at the dinner table, the rules won't last

If you still decide to take it away

There are times when it feels like there is no other option, such as when a child is up using the phone in the middle of the night and can't get up in the morning, or their grades have dropped sharply. In those cases, keeping these points in mind reduces the pushback.

  • Agree on it in advance: when you set the rules, decide together on something like "if I break the rule, I hand in the phone at 8 p.m. the next day". The key is not to take it away in a moment of anger
  • Set a time limit: tell them up front when they will get it back, such as "one day" or "until the weekend"
  • Leave a way to stay in touch: if the child cannot contact family or school, both the child and the parents get anxious. Make sure they can at least make calls
  • Talk when you give it back: when you return the phone, think together about how they could have kept to the rule

For phone use during exam season, see also phone tips for students preparing for entrance exams. If you are unsure what age to give a child a phone, see at what age should a child get a smartphone?

Summary

  • Whether taking the phone away works depends on age and how it is done. Research has found both good and bad results
  • Doing nothing tends to make things worse, while talking things through and close family relationships were linked to less overuse
  • Parents decide while children are young; children decide more as they grow
  • If you take it away, agree on it in advance, set a time limit, and leave a way to stay in touch
  • Something to do today: ask your child, just once, "Is anything about your phone bothering you?"
