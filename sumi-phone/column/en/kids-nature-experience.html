Do children who spend more time in nature like themselves more? What a national survey in Japan shows

November 16, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

Children with more nature experience, like swimming in the sea or rivers and catching insects, tended to have higher self-esteem: the feeling of "I like who I am now." Among children with "a lot" of nature experience, 67.0% had "high" or "fairly high" self-esteem, compared with 32.7% of children with "little." This summary is based on the National Institution for Youth Education's "Survey on Youth Experience Activities and Attitudes."

The short version

  • Children with more nature experience tend to have higher self-esteem, cooperativeness and inquiry skills
  • The pattern held regardless of household income
  • At the same time, participation in nature events run by children's associations and local groups has fallen sharply since fiscal 2006

Nature experience and self-esteem

Self-esteem was measured through answers to statements such as "I like who I am now," "I have my own personality" and "I am confident in my physical strength." The more nature experience a group had, the larger the combined share of "high" and "fairly high."

It was not only self-esteem. Children with more nature experience also tended to score higher on other measures.

Participation in events run by children's associations and neighborhood associations has dropped the most. Meanwhile, the share of children who had nature experiences with family or friends barely changed: 55.2% (fiscal 2006), 57.5% (fiscal 2019) and 56.4% (fiscal 2022). Among children in grades 4-6, the higher the household income, the more nature experience children tended to have. The fiscal 2022 survey asked parents of elementary school children who had not taken part in nature events run by local groups and others why not. In households that spent nothing on education outside school, 15.2% said "it costs money for fees and transport." In households spending 50,000 yen or more a month, it was 4.1%.

Still, the institution says that "regardless of differences in socioeconomic background," children with more nature experience tended to have higher self-esteem. Even without paid events, you can make time to spend in a nearby park or by a river.

Parents' own feelings mattered too. In the fiscal 2019 survey, among children whose parents "strongly agreed" that they were not good at doing activities together with children, 34.0% had had nature experiences with family or friends. Among children whose parents "disagreed," it was 67.0%.

Caveats when reading the numbers

  • The links between nature experience and self-esteem and other measures come from data collected at the same time. They do not show that nature experience raised self-esteem
  • The fiscal 2019 survey was carried out from January to April 2020 at public elementary, junior high and high schools across Japan, with 14,477 children responding
  • The self-esteem and related figures combine answers from grades 4-6, grade 8 (second year of junior high) and grade 11 (second year of high school)
  • The fiscal 2022 figures are as cited in material for a MEXT study group (a presentation by a university researcher)

FAQ

Does nature experience raise self-esteem?

In the National Institution for Youth Education's fiscal 2019 survey, children with more nature experience tended to have higher self-esteem. But because the data were collected at the same time, it cannot show cause and effect.

What counts as "nature experience"?

The survey asked about things like swimming in the sea or rivers, catching insects such as butterflies, dragonflies and grasshoppers, watching wild birds or listening to their calls, and taking time to look at a sky full of stars. Many of these do not require going anywhere special.

Summary

  • 67.0% of children with a lot of nature experience had "high" or "fairly high" self-esteem, compared with 32.7% of those with little
  • The share of elementary school children taking part in nature events run by children's associations and similar groups fell from 33.8% in fiscal 2006 to 9.4% in fiscal 2022
  • Household income makes a difference to experience, but time in nearby nature is still within reach
