What comes after a kids' phone? Four options, including ones that aren't smartphones

October 23, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

What your child should carry after a kids' phone depends not on age but on whether they need LINE (Japan's most-used messaging app) and maps. If they need neither, a 4G feature phone. If they need either one, a smartphone managed by a parent. If you would rather not have social media or games on it from the start, a minimal phone. This article covers when children usually outgrow a kids' phone, how to compare the four options, and how to decide in specific situations such as cram school, club activities and LINE groups.

The short version

  • Most children outgrow a kids' phone somewhere between 5th grade and 7th grade (the first year of junior high). The average age for a first smartphone is 10.2; more than half of 5th graders have one, and more than 80% of 7th graders
  • If calls and SMS are enough, stay with the kids' phone or move to a 4G feature phone. If your child needs LINE and maps, a smartphone or a minimal phone
  • If you choose a smartphone, a parent manages Screen Time (iPhone) or Family Link (Android), and the passcode is not shared with the child
  • Before deciding, sit down with your child and write out a week of "who they contact, and how". That tells you which features are needed

At what grade do children outgrow a kids' phone?

In a November 2025 survey by the NTT Docomo Mobile Society Research Institute (1,200 pairs of elementary and junior high school students and their parents across Japan), children who had a smartphone started using it at an average age of 10.2. Smartphone ownership passes half in 5th grade and 80% in 7th grade. In the same survey, the share of children with a kids' phone but no smartphone was 13% in 4th grade, 11% in 5th grade, 7% in 6th grade and 0% in 7th grade (children with both a smartphone and a kids' phone were counted as smartphone owners).

The Children and Families Agency's fiscal 2025 survey (ages 10 to 17) also shows the share of children who go online with a smartphone rising with each stage of school.

In other words, most families think about what comes "after" the kids' phone somewhere between the upper grades of elementary school and the start of junior high. How to decide the age, and the rules to set before handing over a phone, are covered in at what age should a child get a smartphone? This article is about the next step: which device to choose.

Three common triggers

  • More people to contact: cram school teachers, club teammates, classmates. The stage when contact with family was enough comes to an end
  • Being left out of LINE groups: kids' phones cannot run LINE. If club or class messages go around on LINE, this is where it becomes a problem
  • More places to go alone: taking the train to cram school, a match at an unfamiliar venue. Situations that call for maps and transit directions start to appear

Which of these apply decides what the next device needs. If none of the three applies, there is no rush to switch.

Staying with the kids' phone

A kids' phone is a phone limited to calls, messages and location checks. Even in the upper grades, it is enough if your child mostly contacts family and cram school. There is no need to switch just because "other kids are getting one". If you agree in advance that you will switch when a real need appears, such as "when you join a club" or "when you start commuting by train", your child is more likely to accept it.

4G feature phone (garaho)

A flip phone with physical buttons. It is built around calls and SMS, and there is very little risk of social media or games being installed. You can choose who your child contacts more freely than on a kids' phone, so it is a natural "next device".

Its weak points are LINE and maps. Some models cannot run LINE. LINE's official help page says it plans to end support for old versions of the app (below 14.4.6 on Android) on November 4, 2026, and that devices on Android 7.1.2 or earlier must update to OS 8.0 or later to keep using it. If your child needs LINE, always ask the shop staff before buying: "Will LINE still work on this model after November?" Models and prices are compared in detail in can you still use a feature phone in 2026?

Parent-managed smartphone

Your child can use LINE and maps just like other children. The flip side is that social media, video and games can all be installed too. In the Children and Families Agency's fiscal 2025 survey, junior high school students who use the internet spent an average of about 5 hours 24 minutes online on a weekday, counting smartphones, school devices, game consoles and so on together. If you choose a smartphone, plan on parental management as part of the package.

  • iPhone: create a child's Apple Account through Family Sharing, then on the parent's iPhone go to Settings → Family → your child's name → Screen Time. You can set a daily usage limit (allowed time) and which apps can be used at which times (Screen Time schedules). The parent sets the child's Screen Time passcode on their own iPhone and does not tell the child (on iOS 27)
  • Android: with Google's Family Link, a parent can set a daily usage limit, schedules for downtime and school time, and per-app time limits from their own phone
  • Filtering: by law, when a mobile phone is contracted for a child under 18, the carrier or shop must explain content filtering, and when a smartphone is sold with the contract, they must also set it up (turn it on), unless the parent says it is not needed. Get it set up at the shop when you sign the contract

In the same Children and Families Agency survey, 85.8% of parents of 10- to 17-year-olds who go online with a smartphone managed their child's internet use in some way, and filtering was the most common method (49.2%). But filtering restricts harmful sites and apps; it does not limit time. Manage time with Screen Time or Family Link as above, and treat the settings not as "set and forget" but as something to review each school year.

Minimal phone

A phone with a limited set of features and no social media, games or video. Maps and transit are easier to use than on a 4G feature phone, and it is less distracting than a smartphone. It fills the gap for families who think "we need LINE and maps, but we don't want social media". However, many models come from overseas, so before using one in Japan you must check that it meets Japan's radio law certification (giteki) and whether it runs LINE. The differences between models are covered in comparing minimal phones you can use in Japan.

It does include calls, SMS, maps, transit, weather, a camera, music and disaster-preparedness features. It has a focus mode that silences notifications when you set a time, such as "90 minutes, English vocabulary", and you can let calls from family still come through. Club messages go around in a LINE groupLINEA parent-managed smartphone. A 4G feature phone that supports LINE is also an option
Wants to chat on LINE with classmatesLINE (not urgent)Keep the current device and start by receiving messages through a family member's LINE or the home computer
Entrance exams are coming upCalls, SMS and mapsA device without social media (phone strategies for exam students)

For a child, the club LINE group is something "you're in trouble if you're not in". Even so, there are options besides switching to a smartphone straight away. A parent can receive club messages on their own LINE and pass them on, or you can ask the club's supervising teacher to also send messages by phone or email (how to stay in touch without LINE). LINE with friends is more about fun than about staying in touch. It does not have to be a reason to rush into a new phone.

What to check before switching

  • The school's rules on bringing phones: a July 2020 notice from the Ministry of Education (MEXT) says phones are in principle not allowed in junior high schools, but where a school or board of education allows them, the school, students and parents must agree on four points: making rules, how phones are kept at school and who is responsible if one is lost, parents setting up filtering, and guidance on use. Actual practice varies from school to school, so check your school's guidance first
  • Phone number: can you keep the number that cram school and grandparents already have? Changing it means asking everyone to update their contacts
  • Plan: when you change devices, review the plan too. If it is mostly calls and SMS, a cheap feature-phone plan is enough
  • Location sharing: if checking location was a big part of why you had a kids' phone, make sure the next device can do the same

Something to do today: list who your child contacts

Before you go and look at phones, sit down with your child and write out "who" they contacted this past week, and "how". Lay it out like this: family by phone, cram school by SMS, club on LINE. The features needed become clear.

  • Everything is covered by calls and SMS → the current kids' phone, or a 4G feature phone
  • There is someone they can't reach without LINE → a device that runs LINE. If it is only one club, also try receiving messages through a parent's LINE
  • They will be going alone to unfamiliar places → a device with maps and transit

If you talk it through while looking at the list, you can choose a phone because "this is a real problem" rather than "everyone has one". Children also find it easier to stick to the rules afterward when there is a reason they helped decide.

Summary

  • Children usually outgrow a kids' phone between 5th and 7th grade, the same period when smartphone ownership rises sharply
  • If calls and SMS are enough, stay with the kids' phone or move to a 4G feature phone
  • If your child needs LINE and maps, a parent-managed smartphone or a minimal phone without social media
  • If you choose a smartphone, the parent keeps the passcode and filtering is set up at the shop when you sign the contract
  • Before choosing, write out with your child "who they contact, and how"
