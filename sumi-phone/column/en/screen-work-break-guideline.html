Keep screen work to one-hour stretches and pause for 10 to 15 minutes: what Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare recommends

November 14, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

Work on computers, tablets and smartphones comes with official health guidance in Japan. It is the "Guidelines for Occupational Health Management in Work Using Information Devices," issued by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) in 2019. The target for working time is this: keep each continuous stretch to one hour or less, pause for 10 to 15 minutes before the next one, and stop briefly once or twice within that hour as well. Below we also cover the guidance on eyes and posture, and a government survey on worker fatigue.

What is the guideline?

The guideline was issued on July 12, 2019, by the director-general of the MHLW's Labour Standards Bureau (and partly revised on December 1, 2021). It replaced the earlier "Guidelines for Occupational Health Management in VDT Work," in place since 2002, and sets out what employers should do to reduce the physical and mental strain on workers.

  • Scope: office work using desktop and laptop computers, tablets, smartphones and similar devices
  • Main pillars: managing the work environment, managing the work itself, health management, and health and safety training, among others
  • Remote work: the ministry's leaflet asks employers to follow the guideline for remote workers too, and asks workers at home to use it as a reference

Targets for working time and breaks

ItemWhat the guideline says
Daily working timeShould not be too long
Continuous workNo more than one hour at a stretch
Work pause10 to 15 minutes before the next stretch
Short breaksOnce or twice within each hour, about 1 to 2 minutes each, taken freely rather than at fixed times

A "work pause" here is not the same as an ordinary rest break. The guideline's explanatory notes describe it as time to step away from screen work: to gaze at a distant view, close your eyes, stretch, or do other kinds of work.

The one-hour target is based on research showing that when computer work continues for roughly an hour or more, input errors increase and a measure linked to mental fatigue (the critical flicker frequency) drops.

Targets for eyes and posture

ItemWhat the guideline says
Distance to the screenRoughly 40 cm or more from the eyes; if it is hard to see, correct with suitable glasses
Screen heightTop edge of the screen at about eye level or slightly below
Desk lightingAt least 300 lux on documents and the keyboard; keep the difference between screen brightness and the surroundings as small as possible
GlareUse blinds on windows when sunlight falls on the screen
SittingSit deep in the chair with the soles of your feet flat on the floor; stand up from time to time so you do not stay in one position too long
Tablets and smartphonesFor long sessions, use an external keyboard or similar to prevent fatigue

Eye strain among workers: the government survey

In the MHLW's "Survey on Technological Innovation and Labour" (2008), 68.6% of workers who used computers and similar devices at work said they felt physical fatigue or symptoms from screen work (down from 78.0% in the previous survey in 2003). Asked what those were (multiple answers allowed), the most common was "tired or sore eyes" at 90.8%, followed by "stiff or sore neck and shoulders" at 74.8% and "tired or sore lower back" at 26.9%.

The longer people worked at screens each day, the more of them reported fatigue or symptoms. When asked about their longest stretch of work without a pause of 10 minutes or more, 30.2% said "1 to under 2 hours," 23.1% "2 to under 4 hours" and 9.0% "4 hours or more." Together, more than 60% had days when they worked for an hour or more without a break.

Some notes on reading these figures:

  • The survey covered office, sales and similar workers at private workplaces with 10 or more regular employees (9,107 valid responses, a 63.0% response rate)
  • Fatigue and symptoms were self-reported
  • Because the survey dates from 2008, the devices and ways of working may differ from today. As far as we could find, this is the latest edition of the survey the MHLW has published

FAQ

Is a work pause the same as a rest break?

No. The guideline's explanatory notes say a work pause is time to step away from screen work to look into the distance, close your eyes, move your body or do other tasks, and that it is not an ordinary rest break.

Does this apply to using my phone at home?

The guideline is about screen work done as a job, not a rule for leisure time at home. Still, the idea of "breaking it up into stretches of an hour or less and resting your eyes by looking into the distance" is a useful reference when you spend a long time on screens at home.
