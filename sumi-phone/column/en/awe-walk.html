What is an "awe walk"? What an eight-week experiment found

November 9, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

Standing before a great mountain, a vast sky or a view you have never seen, you can find yourself lost for words. Does walking with an eye out for that feeling of awe change how you feel? A US research team asked 60 healthy people aged 60 to 90 to take a 15-minute walk once a week for eight weeks. The group that walked with awe in mind smiled more broadly in their photos and felt less distress day to day. Here is a summary of the paper.

The short version

  • People who walked with awe in mind felt more awe, joy and warmth toward others during their walks
  • In their selfies, their smiles grew broader and they took up less of the frame over time
  • Scores on tests of anxiety, depression and life satisfaction did not change in either group

How the experiment worked

The study, by Sturm and colleagues at the University of California, San Francisco, was published in the psychology journal Emotion (it first appeared online in 2020). Participants were community volunteers aged 60 to 90 who were followed by the university's healthy aging research network.

Awe walk groupOrdinary walk group
People analyzed24 (average age 75.5)28 (average age 74)
Shared rulesWalk outdoors alone for 15 minutes once a week, at a light to moderate pace. No phone use except for taking photos
Extra instructionsLearned what awe is and that it is most likely in places with vastness and novelty. Go somewhere new each week if possible, and walk with a sense of wonderNone

On each walk, participants took selfies before, during and after, and rated how they felt during the walk. They also recorded their feelings every day, and took tests of anxiety, depression and life satisfaction before and after the study. From the photos, the researchers measured how much of the frame the participant's face and body took up, and how intense their smile was (on a 1–5 scale).

What they found

  • The awe walk group felt more awe during their walks
  • Over time, the awe walkers took up less of their photos and included more of the background scenery (the researchers call this the "small self")
  • They felt more joy and more positive feelings directed toward others, such as compassion, admiration and gratitude, and their smiles in the photos grew broader over the study
  • Even on ordinary days outside the walks, these other-focused positive feelings increased and distress decreased
  • Scores on the anxiety, depression and life satisfaction tests did not change from the start in either group

More walking was not the point

Surprisingly, the ordinary walk group walked much more. And although the rule was 15 minutes, walks actually lasted 38.0 minutes on average (awe) and 46.6 minutes (ordinary). Even so, the ordinary walk group showed no increase in positive feelings or drop in distress. What people paid attention to while walking, rather than how much they walked, may have made the difference. Within the awe walk group, those who took more walks also tended to show larger gains in everyday positive feelings.

Limits of this study

  • It did not test whether the effects lasted beyond the eight-week study
  • Most participants were white and highly educated
  • Participants were healthy and had low anxiety and distress to begin with, which may have made changes in test scores hard to detect
  • For unknown reasons, daily distress was somewhat higher in the awe walk group at the start, so part of the drop may reflect that
  • Only 52 people were analyzed, so it is a small experiment

FAQ

How do you take an awe walk?

In the experiment, people walked outdoors alone for 15 minutes once a week. The awe walk group was told to choose places with a sense of vastness or novelty, go somewhere different each week if possible, and look around with a sense of wonder. Phones were not to be used except for taking photos.

Does it work for younger people too?

This experiment included only healthy people aged 60 to 90, so it cannot tell us whether younger people would see the same effects.

Summary

  • In an experiment where 60 older adults took a 15-minute walk once a week for eight weeks, the group that looked for awe smiled more and felt more positive emotions
  • The ordinary walk group walked more often, but did not show the same changes
  • Anxiety and depression test scores did not change, and it is not yet known whether the effects last
