How to stop checking your phone first thing in the morning: 5 fixes that don't rely on willpower

October 23, 2026 (updated October 25, 2026) · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

The same hand that switches off the alarm opens a notification, and before you know it you've spent 20 minutes in bed. If you can't stop reaching for your phone first thing in the morning, it isn't because your willpower is weak. It's because your alarm and the notifications that came in overnight are all gathered on the phone by your pillow. So the thing to fix isn't you in the morning, but how you prepare the night before. This article looks at why the first few minutes of your morning get taken by your phone, and five fixes you can set up starting tonight.

The short version

  • There are three reasons you check your phone right after waking: your alarm is on the phone, notifications have piled up overnight, and there are a few minutes after waking when you have nothing to do
  • The most effective fix is to get an alarm clock and charge your phone outside the bedroom
  • On top of that, set things up so no notifications come in for 30 minutes after you wake, and decide on one thing to do first when you get up

Why do we check our phones as soon as we wake up?

Checking your phone right after waking is nothing unusual. In a survey by the NTT DOCOMO Mobile Society Research Institute (February 2023, 5,652 smartphone users aged 15 to 79 across Japan), 73% used their phone right after getting up. The highest group, women in their 20s, was at 87%, and even the lowest, men in their 70s, was at 54%. The most common things people do right after waking are shown in the chart below.

The most common is checking email and messages. Morning phone use looks like a matter of willpower, but it's almost entirely a matter of where the phone is and how it's set up. There are three reasons.

  • Your alarm is on your phone: To stop the alarm, you have to pick up the phone. The moment you stop it, the screen is in front of you, and you're already one step from unlocking it. At the time of day when your head is foggiest, you end up holding the tool with the most temptations
  • Notifications have piled up overnight: Messages, news and social media reactions that arrived while you slept are all lined up on the screen in the morning. The more there are, the stronger the urge to "check them," and opening one leads to the next
  • There's nothing to do in the first few minutes after waking: The few minutes in bed before you're fully awake are time with no plan. Unplanned time gets taken by whatever is closest at hand. That's the phone by your pillow

None of these three can be changed by trying harder in the morning. What you can change is deciding the night before where your phone will be, when notifications will arrive, and what you'll do when you get up.

What happens when your phone comes first in the morning

When you open your phone first thing, you spend the first few minutes of your day not on what you decided, but in the order your notifications arrived. Messages you need to think about replying to, news that catches your eye, other people's posts. Before you've even got out of bed, your head is full of other people's business.

Keep looking in bed, and it eats into the time you need to get ready, which leads to a rushed morning. Where late nights cut into your sleep, this cuts into the start of your day from the other end. Phone use at night is covered in "how to stop using your phone before bed." This article focuses on the morning side.

Five fixes you can start tonight

None of these is about "holding back in the morning." They're all ways to set things up the night before so that you can't reach the phone in the morning. They're listed from most to least effective.

1. Get an alarm clock

  • Tonight: Set an alarm clock for the time you want to get up, and turn off your phone's alarm. The clock doesn't need to be expensive, as long as it rings and you can switch it off
  • Why it works: It removes the biggest reason you grab your phone first thing in the morning. Switching off the alarm and opening your phone become separate actions, so you no longer see the screen the instant you wake

It works best for people who rely on snooze. Phone snooze makes you look at the screen every time you hit it.

2. Charge your phone outside the bedroom

  • Tonight: Move the charger outside the bedroom, for example to the living room or hallway. Before bed, leave your phone there and then go into the bedroom
  • Why it works: You can't look at something right after waking if it's out of reach. To get it, you have to get out of bed and leave the room. In that time you wake up and have room to think, "I don't need to look right now"

This assumes you're also using an alarm clock. If your alarm is still on your phone, you can't take it out of the bedroom. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "Sleep Guide for Health Promotion 2023" also says that not bringing smartphones or tablets into the bedroom, and sleeping in as dark a room as possible, leads to good sleep.

3. Make sure no notifications come in for 30 minutes after you wake

  • Tonight: Set your phone's notification-silencing feature to turn on automatically on a schedule. Set it to end not at your wake-up time, but 30 minutes after
  • Why it works: If there's no row of notifications the moment you wake, the urge to "check" is less likely to kick in. You can look at the backlog all at once at a time you choose

Here's how to set it up. The trick is to make it automatic on a schedule rather than turning it on yourself every night.

  • iPhone: The Sleep Focus follows the sleep schedule you set in the Health app, so it ends at your wake-up time. To extend it until 30 minutes after you wake, go to Settings → Focus → Do Not Disturb → Add Schedule, choose a time, and set it to stay on until 30 minutes after your wake-up time. For people you don't want to silence, such as family, choose them under People → Allow Notifications From on the same screen, and their notifications will still come through
  • Android: In Settings, go to Digital Wellbeing → Bedtime mode → bedtime routine, choose to use a schedule, and set the end time to 30 minutes after you wake. If the option to turn off bedtime mode at your next alarm is on, the mode ends when the alarm goes off, so turn that option off. You can allow calls from people you don't want to silence under People in the mode's notification filters. On newer models the setting may be under Settings → Modes → Bedtime, and where things are located varies by model

For tips on sticking with these settings, see also "for people who keep switching off their own Screen Time limits."

4. Decide on one thing to do first when you get up

  • Tonight: Decide on just one thing to do first after waking, write it on a piece of paper and put it next to your alarm clock. Make it something that takes a minute, like "open the curtains," "drink a glass of water" or "wash my face"
  • Why it works: Those empty few minutes in the morning no longer get taken by your phone. If you've decided "when I get up, this comes first," there's no time spent deciding, and your body moves first

If you're unsure, go with "open the curtains." The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "Sleep Guide for Health Promotion 2023" says that getting strong morning sunlight after waking resets the body clock, steadies the sleep-wake rhythm and raises alertness in the brain, and it recommends letting morning sunlight into the room when you wake. If you're looking at your phone in bed, opening the curtains gets pushed back by that much.

Keep the same thing every morning. If it changes from day to day, you have to think about it each time. And while you're thinking, your hand reaches for your phone.

5. Decide when you're allowed to look at your phone

  • Tonight: Pick one time in the morning when it's OK to open your phone, such as "after I finish breakfast" or "the five minutes before I leave home"
  • Why it works: If you ban everything with "no phone in the morning," you end up worrying about urgent messages and feel even less settled. With a set time to look, you have a reason not to look until then. You can also clear the backlog of notifications all at once at that time

On iPhone, under Settings → Notifications → Scheduled Summary, you can set times when notifications from several apps are shown together. If you match a summary time to your "OK to look" time, notifications from the apps in the summary will arrive together at that time.

On mornings when it doesn't work

If you do end up looking at your phone in bed one morning, there's no need to blame yourself. The thing to look back on isn't "was my willpower weak?" but "where was my phone?", "what was my alarm?" and "were notifications coming in?" Usually, one of them has slipped back to the old way.

You don't have to start everything at once. Tonight, just set an alarm clock and leave your phone outside the bedroom. That alone makes the first few minutes of tomorrow morning your own. If you want to rethink your distance from your phone over a few days, also read "how to do a digital detox."

The same idea is why we at Soto Phone are making a phone with no social media, no games and no video apps. Change the tool, so you don't have to rely on willpower. It has calls and a clock, but none of the content that keeps you looking from the moment you wake. Even so, what works best first thing in the morning, with any phone, is leaving it outside the bedroom.

Summary

  • You check your phone right after waking because your alarm, the backlog of notifications and a few minutes with nothing to do are all gathered on the phone by your pillow
  • Get an alarm clock and charge your phone outside the bedroom. This works best
  • Automatically silence notifications until 30 minutes after you wake, and decide on one thing to do first when you get up
  • Once you decide when it's OK to open your phone in the morning, you don't have to look before then
