Feeling anxious when your phone is not with you: how "nomophobia" is measured, and how common it is

November 7, 2026 · By Kazuma Sasaki (Founder, Soto Phone)

On days you leave your phone at home, do you feel a little uneasy? That feeling has a name: "nomophobia", short for "no mobile phone phobia". A 2025 paper that pooled 43 studies from around the world, covering 36,656 people in total, found that 51% had moderate symptoms and 21% had severe symptoms. This summary of the research explains how it is measured and what to keep in mind when reading the numbers.

What is nomophobia?

Nomophobia is the anxiety or discomfort people feel when they cannot use their phone: when they do not have it, the battery is dead, or there is no signal. The 2025 review notes that it is "not formally recognized" in either the DSM-5 (the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual) or the ICD-11 (the WHO's International Classification of Diseases). It is a term used in research rather than the name of an illness.

How is it measured? (the NMP-Q)

The most widely used tool is the NMP-Q, a questionnaire published in 2015 by Yildirim and Correia of Iowa State University in the US. According to a write-up by the British Psychological Society, it was built like this:

  • The researchers interviewed nine university students who relied heavily on their smartphones, and sorted their worries into four themes
  • They wrote 20 questions based on those four themes and tested them with about 300 university students
  • Each question is answered on a 7-point scale for how well it applies to you
The four worriesExample item
Not being able to communicateI would feel nervous because I could not receive calls or messages
Losing connectednessI would feel anxious because I could not check my email
Not being able to access informationBeing unable to get the news on my smartphone would make me nervous
Giving up convenienceIf I could not use my smartphone, I would be afraid of getting stranded somewhere

Total scores run from 20 to 140. A 2023 review of studies on university students uses these bands: 20 means "absent", 21–59 "mild", 60–99 "moderate" and 100 or more "severe". The exact cut-offs vary slightly from study to study.

How many people are affected?

Several papers have now pooled results from many studies (meta-analyses). They all reach a similar picture: about half of people fall in the moderate range, and about one in five in the severe range.

The Jahrami figures are from the paper's abstract. The table in the main text gives 25.8% mild, 52.4% moderate and 20.4% severe. In the 2023 review of university students, the share in the severe range varied widely by country, from 71% in Indonesia to 3% in Germany (each figure comes from a single study).

Things to keep in mind

  • All of these are self-reported questionnaire scores, not a doctor's diagnosis. The 2025 review itself says the figures "reflect self-reported levels of distress rather than clinical diagnoses"
  • Most participants are university students or young adults. In the 2023 review, 26 of the 28 studies came from the Middle East and Asia, and only two from Europe and the Americas
  • Results differ a great deal between studies (statisticians call this high "heterogeneity"). In the 2022 review, 95% of the surveys were conducted online
  • We could not find figures from a large survey of people in Japan

It is not surprising that many people feel a little anxious without their phone, given how much we hand over to it, from messages to maps to payments. FAQ

Is nomophobia a mental illness?

According to the 2025 review, it is not formally included in either the DSM-5 (the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual) or the ICD-11 (the WHO's International Classification of Diseases). It is a research term, and an NMP-Q score is not a diagnosis. If you are struggling for a long time, please talk to a medical professional.

How many questions are on the NMP-Q?

Twenty. Each is answered on a 7-point scale, giving a total score from 20 to 140. It was published in 2015 by Yildirim and Correia.

Summary

  • Nomophobia is the anxiety people feel when they cannot use their phone. It is not an official diagnosis
  • Many studies measure it with the 20-item NMP-Q questionnaire
  • A 2025 review of 43 studies found 51% moderate and 21% severe, but the data are self-reported and come mostly from young adults and university students
