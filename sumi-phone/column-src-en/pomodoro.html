The Pomodoro Technique uses a timer: you work on just one task for a short stretch, then take a break, again and again. It was created by Francesco Cirillo of Italy. In the 1980s, as a university student, he used a tomato-shaped kitchen timer to make himself "focus for just a short time," and that was how it began (pomodoro is Italian for tomato). This article covers the basic method and tips for people who find it hard to stick with.

The short version

  • The basic pattern you often see: 25 minutes of work, then a 5-minute break. After four rounds, take a longer break
  • More important than the timer are "one task at a time" and "writing down interruptions"
  • Put your phone in another room before you start, and use a timer that isn't your phone
  • If 25 minutes feels too long, it's fine to start with 15

The basic method

  1. Write down today's tasks: List the things you want to get done today on paper
  2. Pick one and set the timer: The length you usually see is 25 minutes
  3. Work on that task alone until it rings: If something else comes to mind, jot it on the paper and go back to the task
  4. When it rings, rest for 5 minutes: Stand up and move around. Don't look at your phone
  5. After four rounds, take a longer break: Step away from your desk for about 15 to 30 minutes

The official site describes the method as "more than a 25-minute timer." Using a timer, a pencil and paper, you plan your work, estimate the effort it will take, deal with interruptions, rest, record what happened, and gradually improve the way you work. All of that together is the Pomodoro Technique.

What matters more than the timer: "just one" and "write it down"

  • One task at a time: No writing a report while checking email. Each round is for one task
  • Write down interruptions: When you remember something or someone talks to you mid-round, make a mark on the paper and deal with it later. Once you know how often you were interrupted, you can plan better for the next day
  • Compare estimates with results: Estimate "this task will take two rounds," then write down how many it really took. The more you do this, the more accurate your estimates become

Tips for people who can't stick with it

If your phone keeps winning

  • Don't use your phone as the timer: The moment you stop a phone timer, notifications and social media catch your eye. Use a kitchen timer or a wristwatch instead
  • Put your phone in another room before you start: Just having it on the same desk makes you more easily distracted (see how to stop reaching for your phone while studying)
  • Don't look at your phone during the 5-minute break: Five minutes turning into thirty is the most common mistake. Use the break to stand up, drink some water or look outside

If 25 minutes is too long or too short

  • If you can't stay focused, start with 15 minutes: Build up a run of short sessions you actually finish
  • On a roll? It's OK to keep going past the bell some days: But if you keep skipping breaks, tiredness will hit all at once in the afternoon

If you don't know where to start

  • Use your first round on the smallest task: Something like "open the document and write three headings" is plenty

Using it before exams or for a report

When studying for exams, deciding in advance how many rounds to do each day makes planning easier. For example, "four on weekdays, eight on days off." For how to plan two weeks out, see a pre-exam plan for locking away your phone.

Summary

  • The basics: repeat 25 minutes of work and 5 minutes of rest, with a longer break every four rounds
  • What matters most is one task at a time and writing down interruptions
  • Use a timer that isn't your phone, and put your phone in another room
  • Something to do today: write three tasks on paper and try one round with a kitchen timer
