You set Screen Time to allow "social media for 1 hour a day," yet every time the limit screen pops up, you ignore it and keep going. Plenty of people end up annoyed with themselves over this. But being able to lift a limit you set yourself is not a sign of weak willpower. It is how these tools are built. This article covers the settings that make limits harder to undo, why they still don't stick, and some ways to make them last.

Why you end up switching them off

Out of the box, a limit is only a "reminder"

In its official iOS 26 guide, Apple explains that "by default, Screen Time limits can be ignored when the limit is reached." If you have not set a passcode, you can also change the limit settings themselves straight away. With Android's Digital Wellbeing app timers, too, you can delete the timer from the same settings screen and use the app again right away, without waiting for the midnight reset.

In other words, the default settings only go as far as telling you "you're using this a lot." The one who decides whether to stop is you, at the very moment you most want to keep going.

People want to "tie their own hands in advance"

US economist Hunt Allcott and colleagues ran an experiment with about 2,000 Android users (American Economic Review, 2022). When participants were given a tool that let them set their own daily time limit for each app, their use of six apps, including social media, video and web browsers, fell by about 22 minutes a day on average. Even without any reward, participants chose fairly strict limits for themselves. The research team estimated that roughly 30% of social media use comes from people being unable to stop themselves (a self-control problem).

You are not the only one who wants to cut back but can't. Many people want something that will stop their future selves.

Settings that make limits harder to undo on iPhone

The key is to use a Screen Time passcode that you don't know yourself. If you remember the passcode, the limit ends up being nothing more than "one extra thing to type."

Steps (iOS 27; iOS 26 and earlier in brackets)

  1. Go to Settings → Screen Time → Allowances and set how much time you can use each day for categories such as Social Media and Entertainment. You can see which apps belong to which category under Manage Categories (on iOS 26 and earlier, go to App Limits → Add Limit and choose the apps or categories you want to limit and the time)
  2. Tap Manage Screen Time → Lock Screen Time Settings, and have a family member or friend enter a four-digit code (don't look). From then on, a passcode is needed to change Screen Time settings (on iOS 26 and earlier, scroll down the Screen Time screen to Lock Screen Time Settings)
  3. If you want to set nighttime hours, turn on Schedule under Screen Time Schedule and set the time windows when apps can be used (on iOS 26 and earlier, Downtime → Scheduled. Once a passcode is set, you can turn on Block at Downtime, so that restricted apps cannot be opened during downtime)

Apple's iOS 26 guide gives this Block at Downtime setting as the way to stop limits from being ignored. After setting a passcode, you can also set up Screen Time Passcode Recovery, which lets you reset it with your Apple Account if you forget it. If someone else is choosing your code, decide together with them whether to set this up too.

In iOS 27, the Screen Time screens were redesigned into a Screen Time Schedule for each time window and Allowances for each category. Menu names vary with the iOS version and the device, but the idea is the same: lock the settings with a passcode, and have someone other than you choose the code. That is what works best.

Settings that make limits harder to undo on Android

  • App timers: go to Settings → Digital Wellbeing → App timers, tap the timer icon next to the app you want to limit and choose a time. When you reach the limit, the app closes and its icon turns gray. Timers reset at midnight
  • App limits PIN: go to Settings → Digital Wellbeing → App limits → App limits PIN and set a four-digit PIN, so that only someone with the PIN can change app limits
  • Focus mode: pauses the apps you choose and blocks their notifications while you study or work. It can also turn on automatically on a schedule
  • Bedtime mode: at a set time each night, it can switch the screen to grayscale and silence notifications with Do Not Disturb

The steps and menu names vary slightly from device to device. If you have not set a PIN, you can delete an app timer yourself from the same screen. Focus mode also has a "take a break" option that pauses it for a while. As with iPhone, having someone other than you choose the app limits PIN is the hardest-to-undo approach. For a child's device, parents can also manage it through Google's Family Link. From their own phone, parents can set a daily screen time limit, a schedule for downtime and time limits for individual apps.

Three reasons limits still don't stick

Even after having someone else choose the passcode, some people slide back into old habits after a while. There are three reasons.

1. You still have the right to lift the limit

Economists call arrangements that bind your future self "commitment devices," and sort them into strong and weak ones (Bryan et al., 2010). A limit you can lift yourself is a classic weak one. Even if you hand your passcode to someone else, the commitment is still weak if all it takes is asking them to "unlock it."

2. Exceptions become the norm

Allow "just for today" once, and the next "just for today" comes more easily. Limits last longer if you never make a single exception.

3. Everything is in one device

Even if you limit social media, as long as the same phone has a browser, video and games, you can get back to the same place through another door. A smartphone puts messaging, maps, payments and entertainment all in one device. As long as the things you need and the things that eat your time sit side by side on the same screen, your limits will always get broken "while you're using something you need anyway."

Four ways to make limits last

  1. Have someone else choose the passcode, and don't learn it yourself. If they will unlock it when asked, also agree on rules for when it can be unlocked, such as "only on weekends"
  2. Set limits by time of day, not by length of time. "One hour a day" easily turns into arguments over how it was used, but "no use after 9 p.m. on weekdays" is clear
  3. Delete the app itself, or take it off your home screen. Just not seeing the way in means you open it less often
  4. Leave your phone behind. Keep it in another room while you study, and leave it at home when you go for a walk or head out. Physical distance is the one limit you can't switch off

If you leave your smartphone at home, you don't have to wrestle every day with whether to switch your limits off.

Summary

  • Out of the box, Screen Time and app timers are only "reminders" that you can switch off yourself
  • To make them harder to undo, have someone other than you choose the Screen Time passcode or the app limits PIN, and lock the settings
  • Limits still don't stick because you keep the right to lift them, exceptions pile up, and everything is in one device
  • The strongest limit is physical distance from your phone
