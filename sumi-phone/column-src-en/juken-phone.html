As entrance exams get closer, it is hard to know what to do about your phone. Cancel the contract? Hand it to your parents? Switch to a flip phone? Which choice is right depends on what you end up doing on your phone. This article compares five ways of handling a phone during exam season, then covers what to check before canceling or changing phones, and tips for setting rules together as parent and child.

The short version

  • High school students spend about 6 hours 44 minutes a day online on weekdays (fiscal 2025). If you do nothing, your phone will win out over study time
  • Choose based on what you end up doing. If you cannot stop scrolling social media or watching videos, changing the device works better than making rules
  • Canceling is the last resort. First check that you will not have trouble with exam applications, results announcements, messages from school or juku (private cram schools), or login verification codes
  • For family rules, let the child decide them, set the end date as "the day exams are over", and have parents follow the same rules

How much time do exam students spend on their phones?

In the Children and Families Agency's fiscal 2025 survey, weekday internet use came to about 6 hours 44 minutes a day for high school students and about 5 hours 24 minutes for middle school students. Both are the longest ever recorded in this survey. The figures include use on computers, game consoles and other devices as well as phones.

Meanwhile, study time is going down. In a parent-child survey by the University of Tokyo and the Benesse Educational Research and Development Institute, time spent studying outside school (homework, study at home and juku combined) fell between 2015 and 2025 by 22 minutes a day for high school students and 19 minutes for middle school students. Between 40% and 50% of children now do no home study at all beyond homework.

We cannot say the rise in phone time is the only cause. Even so, there are only 24 hours in a day. For an exam student, phone time competes directly with study time.

Five ways to handle your phone during exam season

Broadly, there are five options. Those near the top are easier; those near the bottom work better but cause more inconvenience.

1. Set rules and keep using it

You set rules such as "no phone after 10 p.m." or "keep it in another room while studying", and otherwise use the phone as normal. This is the easiest option, but also the easiest to break. If your phone time already stays at about one to two hours a day, this is enough. We cover where to keep your phone while studying and how to silence notifications in how to stop picking up your phone while studying.

2. Hand it in at set times

You give your phone to a family member during study time on weekday evenings and weekends. You do not have to stop yourself, so it is more reliable than rules. Decide three things:

  • When it is handed in: set it by the clock, for example "from getting home until 9 p.m."
  • When it comes back: make it "at 9 o'clock", not "when I finish studying". If the condition is vague, you will argue about it every night
  • Exceptions: agree in advance what happens when you need it for juku messages or school assignments

If handing it to family is awkward, another option is a lockable box that will not open until the set time.

3. Delete apps and set limits

You delete social media, video and game apps, and use Screen Time on iPhone or Family Link on Android so they cannot be reinstalled. You can keep what you need, such as calls, maps and school messages.

The weakness is that the limits can be turned off. If you choose the passcode yourself, you will remove the limits yourself when things get hard. This only works if a parent sets the passcode and the student does not know it. We explain why people turn off their limits and how to prevent it in for people who keep turning off Screen Time. Also, as long as a browser is available, you can still see social media and videos even after deleting the apps.

4. Switch to a flip phone or minimalist phone

Until exams are over, you switch to a phone built mainly for calls and SMS. On a model that cannot run social media or video apps, there is nothing to reinstall and no limits to switch off. For people who want to stop but cannot, this is the most effective option.

There are two ways to do it. You can move the SIM from your current smartphone into the basic phone, or you can leave the smartphone at home and get a second phone to carry when you go out. With the second approach, the smartphone can stay with your family, and you use it only for looking things up or doing exam applications (how to carry "two phones" and leave the smartphone at home).

The thing to watch is LINE (Japan's most-used messaging app). Many current 4G basic phones either cannot run LINE or offer only limited features. If your class, club or juku keeps in touch only through LINE, settle on another way to stay in contact before you switch (how to keep in touch without LINE, can you still use a flip phone in 2026?).

5. Cancel the contract

You end the phone contract itself. If you do not have a phone, you cannot touch it. However, canceling means losing your phone number. If you want to keep the number, there are services such as DOCOMO's "Phone Number Storage" that hold your number while you are not using it. Fees and conditions differ by carrier, so check before you sign up.

Canceling works the best, but it also causes the most trouble later. Check the points in the next section before you decide.

What to check before canceling or changing phones

Exam season is a time when a surprising number of messages arrive only by smartphone. Before you switch, check whether each of the following will still work without one.

  • Applications: from the 2026 school year (the exam held in January 2026), applications moved online for the Common Test for University Admissions (Japan's national university entrance exam). You can also apply on a computer or tablet, so a family computer is enough
  • Results announcements: some universities announce results on the web. You can check these on a computer too
  • Verification codes: application sites and juku apps may send a verification code by SMS every time you log in. Keep your number, or change where the codes are sent to email
  • School and juku messages: if messages come only through LINE or an app, ask your teachers to send them by email or phone as well
  • Keeping in touch with family: keep some way to call and text on the way home from juku and on exam day
  • Photos and data: back up your photos and your LINE chat history before canceling or changing phones

Of these, applications and results announcements can be handled on the home computer. What you need when you are out is little more than calls, SMS and maps. Exam students do not need social media in the device they carry around.

Tips for setting rules together as parent and child

A study of schools in England (Beland and Murphy, 2015) found that after schools banned phones, students' results in important exams went up. The biggest gains were among students whose results had been lower to begin with. On the other hand, schools where the ban was not properly enforced showed no clear effect. Rules work when they are kept, not when they are made. The same is true at home.

To make rules that will actually be kept, focus on these five points.

  1. Let the child decide: rules set by parents get broken when parents are not looking. Have the student choose which of the five options to use
  2. Set an end date: decide when it ends, such as "until the day exams are over". When the end is in sight, it is easier to keep going
  3. Write it down and put it up: write the times, places and exceptions on paper and put it where the whole family can see it. That ends arguments over who said what
  4. Parents follow the same rules: if a parent is looking at their phone in the living room while the child's phone is handed in, it will not last
  5. Decide in advance what happens if the rules are broken: instead of scolding, agree that "if the rules are broken, we move to the next option". If rules do not work, hand the phone in; if handing it in does not work, switch phones

One thing for parents to keep in mind is not to stop at simply taking the phone away. For exam students, the phone is also one of the few ways they get a break with friends. Setting a time when the phone is allowed, such as an hour on weekend evenings, makes secret use less likely.

A rough timeline leading up to exams

  • From a year before exams to the summer: start with option 1 or 2. If that does not stick, move to option 3
  • Autumn (three to four months before exams): look at your mock exam results and decide again. If your phone time has not gone down, consider option 4 and switch phones. If you switch, do it before the application process begins
  • The final stretch (one to two months before): do not start anything new. Keep going with your current approach. Do not change phones or cancel your contract at this stage
  • After exams are over: go back to normal on the end date you agreed. That said, anything about your exam-season phone habits that caused no trouble can be kept as it is

Common worries

Will I be left out by my friends if I give up my phone?

During exam season, your friends are just as busy. If you tell them in advance, for example "until exams are over, I'll only check LINE at night" or "call me if it's urgent", you will hardly run into problems. Suddenly replying slowly without telling anyone is more likely to cause misunderstandings. Starting together with a close friend is another option.

What about vocabulary apps and video lessons?

Study apps are the most common reason given for needing a phone. But if you open a vocabulary app and end up opening social media while you are at it, the study app has become a gateway. Keep the screens you study on separate from the ones you play on: watch video lessons on the home computer or a tablet, and use a paper vocabulary book. This assumes the tablet has no social media or video apps on it either.

What should I do on a day I have already given in?

Even if one day falls apart, do not throw out the rules. Go back to the same rules the next day. If things fall apart several times a week, that is a sign the rules do not suit you. Rather than blaming yourself, decide to move one step down to the next of the five options.
