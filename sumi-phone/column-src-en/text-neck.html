"When you look at your phone, your neck is carrying about 27 kg." Have you heard that one? It comes from a paper published in 2014 by Dr. Kenneth Hansraj, a US spine surgeon. But the number is a value calculated with a computer model. Here we look at what the number actually is, what studies have found about neck pain and phone use, and what is still unknown.

The 0° bar in the chart is roughly the midpoint of 4.5–5.4 kg. The paper estimated how the force on the cervical spine grows as the head tilts forward, using a computer calculation method called a "finite element model". A 2017 study presented at the Czech Technical University pointed out that "the model of Hansraj does not assess load at various levels of the cervical spine" and that it does not take the tilt of the whole body (the torso) into account. Its own calculation found that leaning the torso forward 30 degrees can more than double the load on the neck. In other words, the figure is a way to picture "the further your head tilts, the more load on your neck". It is not a measurement of the weight on any one person's neck.

How far do people actually bend their necks?

In a 2015 experiment at a university in South Korea, 18 people texted, browsed the web and watched videos while sitting and standing, and their neck angles were measured. Their heads were bent 33–45 degrees forward from vertical (the median for each condition). The angle was largest when texting, and larger when sitting than when standing.

Neck pain and phone use

  • Overuse and neck pain (2025): pooling seven studies (10,715 people), those who overused smartphones had 2.34 times the risk of neck pain compared with those who did not (odds ratio, 95% confidence interval 1.44–3.82). However, all seven were retrospective studies
  • Sedentary behavior and neck pain (2023): a review of studies in adults found that mobile phone use was linked to neck pain (odds ratio 2.11). But it also found "publication bias" (studies that find a link are more likely to be published), and the authors say longer studies that follow people over time are needed to tell whether it is a cause
  • A study that found no link (2018): among 150 people aged 18–21 in Brazil, there was no clear link between neck posture while texting and neck pain
  • A review of musculoskeletal complaints (2020): across 18 studies, neck and upper back complaints among phone users ranged widely, from 55.8% to 89.9%. The authors write that the evidence is limited because most studies looked at a single point in time

The limits of the research

  • Most studies look at a single point in time, so they cannot tell whether "using a phone caused the pain" or "people who already have pain use their phones more"
  • How "overuse" is defined, and how neck pain is measured, differ from study to study
  • The famous "27 kg" is a calculated value. It does not include the force supported by the neck muscles or differences between levels of the cervical spine

What is clear is that the more time we spend looking at a screen, the more time we spend looking down. Using a phone while walking carries risks beyond your neck, too (see data on accidents from phone use while walking).

FAQ

Is "text neck" a medical diagnosis?

"Text neck" is a term used in research and the media for the head-down posture people adopt when looking at a phone. The studies we reviewed looked at the link between phone use and neck pain; none of them defines it as a diagnosis. If you have neck pain or numbness that does not go away, please see a medical professional.

Is it true that your neck carries 27 kg?

It is a value (60 pounds, about 27 kg) from Dr. Hansraj's 2014 paper, estimating with a computer model the force on the cervical spine when the neck is bent forward 60 degrees. It was not measured on a real person's neck, and critics note that it does not account for factors such as the tilt of the torso.

Summary

  • "About 27 kg at 60 degrees" is a computer-model value. Read it as a picture of how neck load grows the further your head tilts
  • Reviews of the research find a link between phone overuse and neck pain
  • But cause and effect are not clear, and some studies found no link
