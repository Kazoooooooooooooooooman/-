You can live without a smartphone in Japan, even in 2026. For shopping, trains and keeping in touch with family, cards and a phone cover almost everything. The hard parts are three: logging in to your bank, online government procedures, and getting to unfamiliar places. All three can be handled if you prepare in advance. This article goes through everyday situations one by one, looking at how hard each one is and what to use instead.

The short version

  • For payments, contactless credit cards and cash cover almost everything. Code payments such as QR codes make up only about 10% of cashless payment value
  • For trains and buses, a physical transit IC card works fine
  • The thing that needs the most preparation is two-step verification for your bank. Switch it from a smartphone app to SMS or a one-time password card
  • Government procedures can be done with a My Number Card (Japan's national ID card) and a computer with an IC card reader/writer. If your city or town supports it, you can also get a residence certificate at a convenience store using just the card
  • If you do not want to give up maps, transit directions and disaster alerts, the realistic option is to carry one "phone that is not a smartphone"

How hard each situation is, at a glance

In a January 2025 survey by the NTT docomo Mobile Society Research Institute, 98% of people who own a mobile phone used a smartphone. Society is increasingly built on the assumption that "everyone has a smartphone". Even so, when you break it down by situation, the places where it really gets hard are limited.

Let's go through them one at a time.

Payments: cards and cash cover almost everything

According to an announcement by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, Japan's cashless payment ratio in 2025 was 58.0% on the domestic measure (46.3% on the international comparison measure). By value, cashless payments broke down as 82.7% credit cards, 10.2% code payments (QR codes and similar), 3.7% e-money and 3.4% debit cards. So even within cashless, more than 80% of the money goes through credit cards. Without a smartphone, a single card lets you pay at most cashless shops.

  • Convenience stores, supermarkets and chain stores: you can usually pay with a contactless card or a transit IC card
  • Small independent shops, food stalls and shops in rural areas: some still take cash only. Keep a little cash in your wallet
  • Where it gets hard: shops that accept only code payments, and "mobile order only" shops where you order and pay on a smartphone. Check the signs at the register or entrance before you go in

Something you can do today: check whether the credit cards in your wallet support contactless payment (look for a mark like radio waves on the card).

Trains and buses: a transit IC card works fine

With a physical transit IC card (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA and others), you can ride trains and buses without a smartphone. Sales of physical Suica and PASMO cards were suspended for a time in 2023 because of a semiconductor shortage, but registered cards went back on sale on September 1, 2024, and unregistered cards on March 1, 2025 (notice from JR East, the PASMO Association and others). If you use Mobile Suica or similar, buying one card can replace it. You can top up at ticket machines and top-up machines in stations, and also at some shops such as convenience stores (PASMO's guide).

The hard part is looking up routes. For an unfamiliar line or a longer trip, check the route and times on a computer before you go and write them down. Route maps and timetables at the station are also reliable on the spot.

Maps and directions: prepare only for unfamiliar places

You do not need a map for places you usually go. The hard part is going somewhere for the first time.

  • Before you go, open a map on your computer and write down landmarks (the station exit, the building on the corner). Print the map
  • If you get lost, use the area map near the station or ask someone at a shop
  • For meeting up, agree on a precise spot, such as "in front of the ticket gates at the X exit of Y station"

Many people want at least a map with them. In that case, the quickest fix is to choose a simple phone that has maps (can you still use a flip phone in 2026?).

Keeping in touch: calls and SMS are enough

Calls and SMS (messages sent to a phone number) are enough for keeping in touch with family and friends. SMS reaches both smartphones and flip phones, as long as you have the phone number.

The hard part is information shared in LINE groups (LINE is Japan's most-used messaging app). We cover how to keep in touch without LINE, and how to get through difficult situations such as school and club activities, in how to keep in touch without LINE.

One more thing to prepare is a written list of your family's phone numbers. When you rely on a smartphone, you often do not remember the numbers. If you keep them on paper in your wallet, you can call from a public phone even if you lose your phone (we cover things like the number of public phones in how to carry "two phones").

Banking and logins: two-step verification is the biggest hurdle

This is where living without a smartphone tends to be hardest. For online banking, online shopping and logging in to all kinds of services, "two-step verification", where you enter a confirmation code as well as your password, has become the norm. There are three main ways to receive the code.

How you receive itWorks without a smartphone?Key points
A code sent by SMSYesYou can receive it on a flip phone or simple phone too. If you change your number, remember to update your registration
A one-time password card or deviceYesA card or small device issued by the bank that shows a number. You can carry it in your wallet
Approving in a smartphone appNoYou need a smartphone with the app installed. A service that offers only this option cannot be used without a smartphone

For example, Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation offers one-time passwords not only through its smartphone app but also through a one-time password card. You can apply for the card through online banking (SMBC Direct) or at a branch, and the initial registration to start using it can also be done by mail or at a branch. For other banks, you can check whether a card or device is available on each bank's "one-time password" page.

Something you can do today: open the two-step verification settings for your bank and the services you use most (online shopping, your carrier, email), and write down the ones set to "approve in app". Switching them to SMS or a card before you give up your smartphone is the single most important step.

Government procedures: a My Number Card and a computer will do

Procedures that use the My Number Card can be done without a smartphone if you have a computer and an IC card reader/writer (a device that reads the card).

  • Mynaportal (the government's online services portal): to log in on a computer, you need your My Number Card and either an IC card reader/writer or a smartphone that can read the card (Mynaportal FAQ). Without a smartphone, get an IC card reader/writer. To apply on a computer to use the card as your health insurance card, an IC card reader/writer is required, and a smartphone cannot be used instead
  • Tax returns (e-Tax): with a computer and an IC card reader/writer, you can file using the My Number Card method (about the My Number Card method). For the "ID and password method", which does not use the My Number Card, new IDs and passwords stopped being issued on October 1, 2025 (National Tax Agency notice), and the method is scheduled to become unusable from tax returns for the 2027 tax year (National Tax Agency e-Tax). If you want to keep using e-Tax without a smartphone, get the card and a reader/writer ready
  • Residence certificates and other documents: if your city or town supports it, you can use your My Number Card to get a copy of your residence certificate or a seal registration certificate from the multi-function copier at a convenience store (convenience store issuance). No smartphone is needed

And of course, if you go to the counter at a government office, you can do the procedure with neither a smartphone nor a computer. Going online is handy when you cannot find time to visit the counter.

Work and school apps: check whether there is a computer version

Workplace chat, clocking in and out, and school messaging apps sometimes assume you have a smartphone. Many also have a browser version for computers, but some features, such as clocking in or reporting attendance, may only work in the smartphone app.

  • Check whether the apps you use have a computer or browser version
  • For things that only work in the smartphone app, ask your workplace or school whether there is another way (computer, phone, paper)
  • Some companies lend out work devices. Explain why you do not carry a personal smartphone and ask

This varies a lot from workplace to workplace and school to school. If there really is no alternative, one option is to keep a smartphone for work only, left at home or at work.

Disasters and emergencies: have one way to receive alerts

Emergency alerts such as Earthquake Early Warnings, tsunami warnings and evacuation information can be received on a compatible phone, even if it is not a smartphone. docomo's Area Mail needs no sign-up and is free, including data charges. However, only a handful of feature phones (mobile phones other than smartphones) are on docomo's list of compatible models, and most of them are no longer made. Check the list before you buy.

  • When choosing a phone, always check that it supports emergency alerts
  • Keep a battery-powered radio at home
  • Write down your family's numbers and your evacuation site on paper, and keep copies in your wallet and at home

Living with no mobile phone at all leaves the most worry when it comes to information and contact during a disaster. Even if you give up your smartphone, we recommend keeping one phone that can make calls and receive alerts.

How to start living without a smartphone

  1. Switch your two-step verification: change anything set to "approve in app" to SMS or a one-time password card
  2. Get your cards in order: a contactless credit card and a physical transit IC card. Carry a little cash too
  3. Set up your computer for procedures: if you will use your My Number Card, get an IC card reader/writer
  4. Write numbers down on paper: keep the numbers for family, work and school in your wallet
  5. Try it on weekends first: leave your smartphone at home when you go out, and note down anything that causes trouble. Then fix those problems one at a time

You do not have to cancel your contract right away. Another option is to leave your smartphone at home and carry a different phone when you go out (how to carry "two phones" and leave your smartphone at home).
