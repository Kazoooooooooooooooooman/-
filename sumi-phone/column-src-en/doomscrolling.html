What doomscrolling means

The word joins "doom" and "scroll". The American dictionary Merriam-Webster defines it as spending excessive time scrolling through online content (especially news) that makes one feel sad, anxious, angry, and so on. According to the dictionary, the first known use was in 2020.

2020 was the year of COVID, and the word spread fast.

  • Australia's Macquarie Dictionary chose doomscrolling as its 2020 Word of the Year (Committee's Choice)
  • In New Zealand, it was voted word of the year for 2020 in a public poll run by the website Public Address (as reported by RNZ)

The Macquarie Dictionary describes it as continuing to read news feeds online or on social media even though the news is mostly negative and often upsetting.

The experiment: 2–4 minutes of grim news

In 2021, a team including researchers from the University of Essex in the UK ran two experiments. Participants were randomly split into three groups.

  • A group that saw COVID news
  • A group that saw stories about "acts of kindness" during the pandemic
  • A group that saw nothing

In Study 1 (299 people), participants viewed a Twitter feed for at least 2 minutes. In Study 2 (603 people), they watched a YouTube video for 4 to 4.5 minutes. In both studies, people who saw COVID news reported lower positive mood than people who saw nothing. In Study 2, their optimism about the future also dropped.

In the group that saw acts of kindness, positive mood did not drop. In Study 2, their negative mood was actually a little lower. The researchers think the problem is not social media itself, but what you look at.

There are caveats. Most participants were young, and the samples skewed white and female. The studies also measured mood right after viewing. They cannot tell us about long-term effects.

What daily tracking found

A team including researchers from the University of Vermont in the US asked 61 people aged 18 to 20 to answer a survey every day for 30 days during the pandemic in 2020. On days when they saw more pandemic-related social media, their symptoms of depression and PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder) tended to be stronger. This link was stronger in people who had more difficult experiences in childhood. The same link was not seen for traditional media such as TV and newspapers.

However, the study also found that people with more severe symptoms to begin with used more social media. With only 61 people, we cannot say that viewing social media made the symptoms worse.

What surveys found

A research team in Türkiye built a scale to measure how much people doomscroll (2023). In a survey of 460 people, those who doomscrolled more reported more psychological distress, and through that, lower life satisfaction and other wellbeing measures. Another Turkish survey (400 people, 2024) also found a link between doomscrolling and poorer mental wellbeing.

Both are one-time surveys, so the reverse is also possible: people who are struggling may be the ones drawn to bad news. Both also only cover adults in Türkiye.

FAQ

What does doomscrolling mean?

It means scrolling on and on through online news or other content that makes you feel sad, anxious, or angry, without being able to stop. Merriam-Webster gives 2020 as the word's first known use.

Is doomscrolling bad for your mental health?

In a UK experiment, just 2–4 minutes of COVID news lowered people's positive mood. Surveys have also found links with psychological distress. But the long-term effects, and which way cause and effect run, are still unclear. If you keep feeling low, please talk to a support service or a doctor.
