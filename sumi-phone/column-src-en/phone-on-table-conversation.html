When you talk with a friend in a cafe, do you leave your phone on the table? Even if you don't touch it, its mere presence may make the conversation shallower. Studies making that claim began appearing around 2012 and drew a lot of attention. But a 2021 replication did not find the same result. Based on the papers' abstracts and articles describing the research, here are both sides.

The short version

  • In experiments from 2012–2013, just having a mobile phone nearby lowered how close two strangers felt to each other
  • In an observational study in cafes, conversations with a phone on the table were also rated lower than those without
  • But a 2021 replication did not find the same result, and its authors say the effect, if it exists, is hard to find

The first experiments (2012–2013)

Two researchers, Przybylski and Weinstein, ran two experiments. The paper appeared online in 2012 and in the journal in 2013.

  • Pairs of strangers talked for 10 minutes in a private booth
  • For half the pairs, a mobile phone was placed on a table nearby. For the other half, a notebook was placed in the same spot. In both cases it was outside their direct line of sight
  • Afterward, participants rated how close they felt to their partner, how much they could trust them, and so on

Pairs with a phone nearby tended to feel less able to form a close relationship with their partner. In the second experiment, the topic was either a mundane one or "the most meaningful events of the past year." According to the paper's abstract, the negative effect was clearest when people discussed the meaningful topic.

According to a write-up from the British Psychological Society, participants did not seem to be aware that the phone had any effect. The 2021 replication paper notes that the original experiments used a "cellphone," while the replication used a smartphone.

The "iPhone Effect": 100 pairs observed in cafes (US)

Does the same thing happen outside the lab, in everyday places? A team at Virginia Tech in the US studied this in cafes in and around Washington, DC. The paper appeared online in 2014 and in the journal in 2016.

  • 100 pairs who knew each other talked for 10 minutes. Each pair was randomly assigned either a casual topic or a meaningful one
  • A research assistant watched from a distance and noted whether either person put a phone or other device on the table or held it
  • 29 pairs had a device present and 71 did not (according to a summary by The Journalist's Resource)

Conversations without a device were rated higher, even after accounting for differences in age, gender, ethnicity and mood. People also felt more strongly that their partner tried to understand their thoughts and feelings. And among pairs who were already close, having a phone present lowered this sense of being understood more than it did for less close pairs.

There are some caveats:

  • The researchers did not decide who had a phone out; they only observed whether people put one there. The British Psychological Society write-up points out that people may have reached for their phones because their partner was less engaging
  • The differences were small, about 0.3 to 0.4 points on a 7-point scale
  • The topic (casual or meaningful) made no difference, unlike in the first experiments

A 2021 replication could not reproduce it

Two researchers at HEC Paris in France repeated the first experiments as closely as they could. In 2018, a total of 356 people (136 groups) took part at a European research lab. Differences from the original included using an ordinary smartphone and adding creativity tasks after the conversation.

The result: whether a smartphone or a notebook was on the table made no difference to how people felt about their relationship with their partner. This was true in both of their experiments. The authors conclude that the effect of a phone's mere presence is "at minimum harder to find than what was previously assumed."

The paper also reports that a 2020 analysis combining six studies (a meta-analysis) found the effect overall unsupported. At the same time, the authors write that a null result does not prove there is no effect. They suggest that factors such as a young sample used to phones, and using a lab phone rather than participants' own phones, may have played a part.

Research on phones on the desk and studying or memory has followed a similar path, with the original study and later replications disagreeing. See Does a phone on your desk dull your mind? The "brain drain" research since.

What the research tells us, and what it doesn't

  • What we know: some studies have found a link between a phone on the table and the quality of a conversation
  • What we don't know: whether the phone actually causes the conversation to become shallower. The replication did not reproduce the effect
  • Every study rated a conversation of about 10 minutes with a questionnaire afterward. None looked at effects on long-term relationships

There is no clear answer yet. Still, putting your phone in your bag when you have something important to talk about takes no effort and costs you nothing.

FAQ

Does just putting a phone on the table lower the quality of a conversation?

Some studies found it did (the 2012–2013 experiments and the 2014 cafe observation study), and one found no difference (the 2021 replication). The team behind the 2021 replication says the effect is at minimum harder to find than previously assumed. For now, there is no clear conclusion.

What is the "iPhone Effect"?

It is the title of a study by a Virginia Tech team that observed 100 conversations in cafes. Conversations where a phone or other device was on the table or in someone's hand scored lower on feeling connected and feeling understood than those without. However, people chose for themselves whether to have a phone out, so the study cannot show that the phone was the cause.

Summary

  • In the 2012–2013 experiments, just having a mobile phone nearby lowered how close two strangers felt
  • In the 100-pair cafe study, conversations with a phone present were rated lower, but the difference was small and cannot be called a cause
  • A 2021 replication with 356 people did not reproduce the effect, and research continues
