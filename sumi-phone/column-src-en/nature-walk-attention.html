When your mind is worn out from concentrating, a walk in nature brings your focus back. That idea is called Attention Restoration Theory. In a 2008 experiment at the University of Michigan, students who walked in a park for about 50 minutes improved more on an attention test than after a walk downtown. Later reviews of the research, however, found effects on only some tests, and the effects were small to moderate. Here is what the experiment did and where its limits lie, based on the papers.

The short version

  • In the 2008 experiment, attention test scores rose clearly only after the park walk
  • Later reviews also found small to moderate effects on tests of memory and mental flexibility
  • But the effect does not show up on every test, and many of the studies are small

What is Attention Restoration Theory?

Attention Restoration Theory is an idea put forward by Stephen Kaplan of the University of Michigan, who was also one of the authors of the 2008 paper (Kaplan, 1995). It holds that the attention we steer on purpose when studying or working ("stay focused, don't look away"), called directed attention, is a limited resource that runs down as we use it.

The 2008 paper explains the difference between nature and the city like this:

In natureIn the city
What reaches your eyes and earsThings like trees and water that gently catch your attentionCars, people and signs that grab your attention hard
Directed attentionLittle of it is neededNeeded constantly, for example to avoid being hit by a car
What the theory predictsAttention recovers more easilyAttention recovers less easily

The 2008 experiment: about 50 minutes in a park and downtown

Experiment 1 involved 38 University of Michigan students (23 women and 15 men, average age 22.6). Here is how it worked:

  • First, they took a "backwards digit span" test: they heard a string of digits and had to repeat it in reverse order. The maximum score was 14
  • Next, they spent 35 minutes on a task designed to tire their minds further
  • They then walked for 50 to 55 minutes, either in a park near campus (a tree-lined arboretum, away from traffic and people) or downtown (along a busy main road). Both routes were 2.8 miles (about 4.5 km)
  • Back in the lab, they took the same test again
  • A week later, they did the same thing on the other route (the order was switched between participants)

After the park walk, scores rose by 1.5 points on average; after the downtown walk, by 0.5 points. The gain in the park was statistically clear, but the gain downtown was not. The paper notes that simply getting used to taking the test twice cannot explain the difference.

Participants were also in a better mood after the park walk, but changes in mood were not related to changes in test scores. The month of testing (September, November, January or July) made no difference either.

Is looking at pictures enough? (Experiment 2)

In Experiment 2, 12 students looked at either 50 pictures of nature or 50 pictures of cities for about 10 minutes. On a test that breaks attention into separate parts, only the ability to ignore distractions (executive attention) improved, and only after the nature pictures.

However, on the backwards digit span test in Experiment 2, the difference between nature and city was not statistically clear. With only 12 people, this result should be read cautiously.

What later reviews found

The 2008 experiment is widely cited, but one experiment cannot settle the question. Two reviews have since pooled many studies.

In other words, there is evidence pointing toward "time in nature restores some aspects of attention a little." But it does not support the claim that "a walk in nature will always sharpen your focus."

Limits of the research

  • The 2008 experiments involved only 38 and 12 students from a single university
  • What was measured was test scores right after the walk. The study did not look at whether schoolwork or job performance improved
  • Before walking, participants spent 35 minutes deliberately tiring their minds. It is not known whether the same effect appears when people are not already tired
  • The experiment did not look at walking while using a phone

FAQ

How long should I walk in nature?

In the 2008 experiment, participants walked for 50 to 55 minutes, about 4.5 km. The reviews do not pin down how much time is needed for an effect. Treat the length used in this experiment as one rough guide.

Does just looking at nature photos or videos help?

In Experiment 2 of the 2008 study, one part of attention improved after about 10 minutes of looking at nature pictures. But it was a small experiment with 12 people. The 2018 review suggests real nature may work better than virtual nature, while noting this could be due to differences in exposure time.

Summary

  • Attention Restoration Theory holds that nature lets the attention we steer on purpose rest
  • In the 2008 experiment, attention test scores rose by 1.5 points on average after a roughly 50-minute park walk (0.5 points downtown)
  • Later reviews found effects on only some tests, and the effects were small to moderate
