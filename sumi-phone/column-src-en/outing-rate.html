The share of people who went outside on the survey day (the outing rate) was 74.1% on weekdays and 52.5% on days off in 2021. Both are the lowest since the survey began in 1987. On days off, nearly 1 in 2 people stayed at home all day. This summary is based on the Nationwide Person Trip Survey by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT).

The short version

  • Outing rate: 74.1% on weekdays and 52.5% on days off (in 1987: 86.3% on weekdays and 69.5% on days off)
  • Trips per person per day: 1.96 on weekdays and 1.47 on days off, also record lows
  • Since 2015, people in their 20s have made fewer trips than people in their 70s, and the gap widened further in 2021

MLIT notes that the 2021 survey was affected to some degree by COVID-19 (it was carried out before the Omicron wave and outside periods such as states of emergency). Even so, the rate had already dropped sharply by 2015.

People in their 20s move less than people in their 70s

  • Weekday trips by people in their 20s fell from 2.74 in 1987 to 1.69
  • People in their 70s went from 1.53 to 1.97, overtaking people in their 20s in 2015
  • On days off too, people in their 20s made 1.26 trips against 1.53 for people in their 70s
  • Among people in their 20s, men made fewer weekday trips for commuting and work, and women made fewer day-off trips for shopping and the like

MLIT says that a higher share of people in their 20s than in their 70s have moved their activities online. Shopping, and even talking with people, can now be done on a screen.

Give yourself a reason to go out

  • Make just one plan on your day off: a walk, a public bath (sento), the library. Somewhere nearby is fine (how to keep your day off from disappearing into your phone)
  • Walk a little farther: even walking one extra train stop adds to your time outside (the benefits of walking)

Summary

  • The outing rate is 74.1% on weekdays and 52.5% on days off, the lowest since the survey began
  • The number of trips is also at a record low. People in their 20s now move less than people in their 70s
  • COVID-19 played a part, but the decline had already begun by 2015
