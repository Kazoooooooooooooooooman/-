Does your mood change if you cut back on social media or stop for a while? Several randomized experiments, in which participants are assigned to groups by chance, have found that loneliness and low mood went down. But the effects tend to be small, and some studies found no effect or even the opposite. Here are the best-known experiments and a review of 21 studies, with their numbers and limits.

The short version

  • Experiments that cut or stopped social media found reductions in loneliness, low mood and anxiety
  • But the effects are fairly small, and most studies lasted only one to four weeks
  • A review of 21 studies found a mix of positive effects, no effects and negative effects

A limit of about 30 minutes a day: 143 students (2018)

In an experiment at the University of Pennsylvania (Hunt et al., 2018), researchers tracked 143 students' normal use for a week and then randomly split them into two groups.

  • Limited group: Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat capped at 10 minutes each per day (about 30 minutes in total)
  • Comparison group: use social media as usual
  • Duration: three weeks. Use was checked with screenshots of the iPhone battery screen

After three weeks, the limited group showed clear reductions in loneliness and low mood compared with the usual-use group. The effect was largest for those who were more depressed at the start. Anxiety and "fear of missing out" (FOMO) fell slightly in both groups; the researchers think simply tracking one's own use may have helped.

There are limits. Participants were students at a single university, aged roughly 18 to 22, and only three platforms were studied. The study does not tell us the ideal number of minutes.

Four weeks without Facebook: 1,661 people (2020)

A team of economists from Stanford University and other institutions (Allcott et al., 2020) asked people to deactivate their Facebook accounts for the four weeks before the 2018 US midterm elections. Participants were recruited through Facebook ads, and the effects were analyzed for 1,661 people. More than 90% of the deactivation group complied.

  • Deactivation freed up an average of 60 minutes a day, and people spent more time on offline activities such as watching TV alone and seeing family and friends
  • The index of subjective well-being improved by 0.09 standard deviations: small but statistically clear, with improvements in particular in happiness, life satisfaction, depression and anxiety
  • Several weeks after the experiment ended, the deactivation group was still using the Facebook app about 11 minutes (22%) less than the comparison group
  • About 80% of the deactivation group said deactivating was good for them

The researchers point out the caveats themselves. Participants were younger, better educated and more left-leaning than the average Facebook user, and only people who used Facebook more than 15 minutes a day were included. Many outcomes were self-reported, and results might differ for a different length or timing of deactivation (this one was just before an election).

A one-week break from social media: 154 people (2022)

In an experiment at the University of Bath in the UK (Lambert et al., 2022), 154 people (average age 29.6) were randomly split, and one group stopped using Facebook, Instagram, Twitter and TikTok for a week. After one week, the break group reported higher well-being and lower depression and anxiety than the usual-use group. The researchers say the findings still need to be tested in clinical populations and over longer periods.

A review of 21 studies: mixed results (2022)

The review by Radtke et al. (2022) gathered studies of "digital detox," meaning time away from smartphones, social media or messaging apps. It covered 21 studies with 3,625 participants in total, 12 of which were randomized controlled trials.

The results varied from study to study. For health and well-being, relationships, self-control and performance, some studies found positive effects, others found no effect, and some even found that well-being got worse. The review calls for more research into why the results differ. For practical approaches, see how to do a digital detox.

FAQ

Does quitting social media really make you happier?

Randomized experiments have found that cutting or stopping social media reduced loneliness and low mood. But the effects are fairly small (in the four-week Facebook experiment, the well-being index improved by 0.09 standard deviations), and the review of 21 studies also found studies with no effect or the opposite effect. We cannot say it will always help.

Do I have to quit completely?

Some experiments found benefits without quitting. In the 2018 study, simply limiting three platforms to about 30 minutes a day in total reduced loneliness and low mood. For ways to step back without deleting your accounts, see what social media fatigue is and how to deal with it.

Summary

  • Students who limited social media to about 30 minutes a day saw loneliness and low mood fall within three weeks
  • People who stopped Facebook for four weeks gained 60 minutes a day, felt slightly better, and used it less afterward
  • But the studies were short, and a review of 21 studies found mixed results
