You feel your phone buzz in your pocket, check the screen, and find nothing there. Sound familiar? This is known as "phantom vibration syndrome". In a survey published in the British medical journal BMJ in 2010, 68% of staff at a US hospital said they had experienced it. The short version

  • In many studies, more than half of people have felt a phone vibrate or ring when it did not
  • One study found it increased during a period of heavy workload and stress, and dropped once things calmed down
  • Few people are bothered by it, and there are no criteria for treating it as an illness yet

What is phantom vibration syndrome?

It is the sensation that your phone, or a pager, is vibrating when it is not. Despite the word "syndrome", it is not a medical diagnosis. A 2015 review of the literature (Deb) notes that many people experience it but few find it bothersome, and that criteria for identifying it as a clinical condition have yet to be developed. When it is a sound you think you hear, it is called "phantom ringing".

How common is it?

  • US (2010): 176 hospital staff responded. Of the 169 who answered the question, 115 (68%) had experienced it, and 13% felt it every day
  • Taiwan (2013): a study that followed 74 medical interns for a year. 78.1% before the internship, 95.9% in month 3, 93.2% in month 6, 80.8% in month 12, and 50.0% two weeks after the internship ended
  • Iran (2017): 54.3% of 380 medical sciences students. 49.3% had heard a phantom ring
  • Italy (2019): 58.9% (1,685) of 2,859 school students aged 10–14 who owned a smartphone. This figure combines phantom vibrations and phantom ringing

The studies looked at people of different jobs and ages and asked their questions in different ways, so the numbers cannot simply be compared side by side. Still, every one of them is above half.

Why does it happen?

No one knows the exact cause yet. Studies point to the following:

  • Being on alert: the Taiwanese study suggests that when you are tense and waiting to be contacted (on high alert), your brain may be more likely to read a small sensation as a "vibration", and that stress strengthens this effect
  • How you carry and use the phone: in the 2010 US survey, people who kept the device in a breast pocket (compared with on a belt), carried it for more hours, and used vibrate mode more often were more likely to experience it
  • Workload and fatigue: among the Taiwanese interns, it rose during the demanding internship and fell once it ended. In a survey of 384 employees at a tech company in Taiwan, it was linked to work-related fatigue (burnout)

On the other hand, the study of Taiwanese interns found no clear link with anxiety or depression scores. The Italian study of children found a link with emotional problems, but said it could not tell whether this was a cause or something that simply occurs alongside it.

Can you make it stop?

In the 2010 US survey, 39% of those who had experienced it said they had been able to stop it. The strategies they tried, and how often each worked:

StrategyShare who stopped it
Turning off vibrate mode75%
Carrying the device somewhere else63%
Switching to a different device50%

FAQ

Is phantom vibration syndrome an illness?

It is not a medical diagnosis. The 2015 literature review says that while many people experience it, few are bothered by it, and there are no criteria yet for identifying it as a clinical condition. If you feel strong anxiety or distress, please talk to a medical professional.

Is it linked to phone addiction?

That is not clear. A 2014 study of 283 people in Taiwan (average age 22.9) found little or no link between scores on a smartphone addiction questionnaire and phantom vibration or ringing. For a rough guide to addiction, see our phone addiction self-check.

Summary

  • Depending on the study and the period, between half and over 90% of people have felt a phone vibrate when it did not
  • Workload, stress, use of vibrate mode and where you carry the phone appear to play a role
  • It is not a diagnosis, and few people are bothered by it. Turning off vibrate or carrying the phone elsewhere are among the strategies people have tried
