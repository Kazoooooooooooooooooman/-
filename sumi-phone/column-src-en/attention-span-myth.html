But when a BBC statistics program traced the number back to its source, it found no research behind it. Here is how the number spread, what researchers think, and what we actually know about phone notifications and focus.

According to the BBC, the number has appeared in many outlets, including Time magazine and newspapers in the UK, the US and Canada.

Tracing it back

In 2017, the BBC World Service statistics program More or Less looked into where the number came from.

  • All the references lead back to a 2015 report by the Consumer Insights team of Microsoft Canada. The report was based on a survey of 2,000 Canadians and on measuring the brain activity of 112 people
  • But the "8 seconds" figure did not come from Microsoft's own research. It was quoted from a website called Statistic Brain
  • Statistic Brain listed the National Center for Biotechnology Information at the US National Library of Medicine and the Associated Press as sources. When contacted, neither could find any record of research backing up the figures
  • Attempts to contact Statistic Brain itself also came to nothing

In other words, a number that spread around the world had no verifiable research at its root.

What researchers think

Speaking to the BBC, Dr Gemma Briggs, who studies attention at the Open University in the UK, said she does not think it is true that attention spans are getting shorter. Psychologists simply do not try to measure attention that way. She said the idea of an "average attention span" is pretty meaningless: "It's very much task-dependent."

As for goldfish, Prof Felicity Huntingford, who has studied fish behavior for almost half a century, said: "Goldfish can perform all the kinds of learning that have been described for mammals and birds." Goldfish have long been used in research on memory and learning.

A similar claim says that attention in lectures runs out after 10 to 15 minutes. A 2016 commentary in a US physiology education journal traced this claim back through the papers usually cited for it. It found that the most often cited source barely discusses student attention at all, and that the available data do not support a 10- to 15-minute limit. It also noted that differences in attention come more from differences between teachers than from the teaching format.

What we know about phones and focus

Even if the "8-second attention span" has no basis, that does not mean phones never get in the way of focus. In 2015, a team at Florida State University in the US tested what happens when people get phone notifications (sounds or vibrations) while doing a task that needs attention.

Notifications alone clearly hurt performance on the task, even though participants did not touch their phones. The drop was about as large as when people were actually on a call or texting. The researchers think even brief notifications can trigger thoughts unrelated to the task, pulling the mind away from it.

Attention is not a fixed number of seconds. It changes with your surroundings. Cutting back on notifications is one of the easiest things you can do. See which notifications to keep and which to turn off. For phones in class, the PISA 2022 survey is also worth a look.

FAQ

Is the human attention span really 8 seconds?

There is no verified basis for it. The BBC found that "8 seconds" was a number the 2015 Microsoft Canada report quoted from another website, and no original study could be found. Researchers say how long we pay attention depends on the task.

Is it true that a goldfish's attention span is 9 seconds?

According to the fish researcher the BBC spoke to, there is no evidence that goldfish have especially short attention spans or memories. In fact, goldfish have long been used in research on memory and learning.
