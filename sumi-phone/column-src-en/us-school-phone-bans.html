In the United States, state laws and policies restricting phones in schools have spread fast. According to a tally by the education newspaper Education Week (updated October 26, 2026), at least 40 states and Washington, D.C. require school districts to ban or restrict students' phones. Of these, 30 states and D.C. use "bell-to-bell" rules: phones cannot be used from the first bell of the day to the last.

The short version

  • As of October 2026, at least 40 states and D.C. require schools to ban or restrict phones (Education Week)
  • The most common type is "bell to bell", which also covers breaks and lunch
  • 72% of US high school teachers said phones in class were a "major problem"

An example: New York State

On May 6, 2025, New York announced bell-to-bell restrictions as part of its state budget agreement. They took effect at the start of the 2025–26 school year (fall 2025).

  • They cover K-12 public school districts and charter schools
  • Students may not use smartphones without permission for the whole school day, including lunch and study hall, not just in class
  • Exceptions include managing a medical condition, when required by an IEP, translation, family caregiving and emergencies
  • Students may carry simple phones that cannot connect to the internet, and may use school-issued laptops and tablets

It is interesting that even with the smartphone put away, a phone without internet access is allowed. The idea is to keep contact with family while stepping away from smartphone notifications and social media.

How do teachers feel?

In a fall 2023 survey of K-12 teachers by the US research organization Pew Research Center, 72% of high school teachers, 33% of middle school teachers and 6% of elementary school teachers said that students being distracted by phones was a major problem in their classroom.

Things to keep in mind

  • The Education Week numbers are a count by journalists who researched state laws and policies. They are not official government statistics
  • They count what states require of districts. How well the rules are followed in individual schools is a separate question
  • Some other states encourage or fund local bans without requiring them; these are not included in the count
  • Effects on grades and attendance are not yet clear. Education Week also reports on a study suggesting that school phone restrictions do not seem to boost achievement or attendance much
