You watch a video, reply to messages on your phone, and open another app while you're at it. A 2009 study from Stanford University in the US reported that people who do a lot of this "media multitasking" are more easily distracted by irrelevant information, and it drew wide attention. Later studies, however, sometimes found the same result and sometimes did not. Based on the papers, here is what we know and what we don't.

The short version

  • In the 2009 study, students who media multitasked more were more easily distracted by irrelevant information
  • Later studies are mixed, and many find no difference
  • We still don't know whether multitasking makes people more distractible, or whether people who are already more distractible tend to multitask

The 2009 Stanford study

The research team first surveyed 262 university students. For 12 types of media, including TV, video, music, games, phone calls, email and the web, students reported how often they used other media at the same time while using each one. From this, the team calculated a score for how much each person media multitasks.

They then picked people well above average (heavy media multitaskers) and well below average (light media multitaskers) and gave them several computer tests. Each test had 15 to 22 people per group.

  • A test of ignoring distracting shapes: as more irrelevant shapes appeared on screen, heavy multitaskers did worse. Light multitaskers were unaffected
  • A memory test (remembering letters shown a little earlier): when the test got harder, heavy multitaskers more often wrongly said an irrelevant letter had appeared
  • A task-switching test: when going back and forth between two tasks, heavy multitaskers needed 167 milliseconds more of extra switching time

On the other hand, there was no difference on a test of stopping an action on the spot, or on a version of an attention test without distractions. The team suggested that heavy multitaskers may be less able to filter out irrelevant information.

Later studies are mixed

After 2009, many studies on similar questions followed. In 2018, a team including one of the 2009 study's authors reviewed this research (a review paper in PNAS).

  • Memory (working memory): of 24 tests in adults, about half found heavy multitaskers did worse. The rest found no difference. No test found heavy multitaskers did better
  • Task switching: of 10 tests, heavy multitaskers did worse in 3, there was no difference in 5, and heavy multitaskers did better in 2
  • Ignoring distracting shapes: this was the central result of the 2009 study, but most of the data showed no difference

The review notes that heavy multitaskers tend to fall behind mainly on tasks that require keeping attention going. It suggests that occasional lapses of attention may explain many of the results.

Replications and a meta-analysis

A paper published in 2017 repeated the 2009 study with university students in Indonesia and the Netherlands. Across the two replications there were 14 tests in total. Only 5 showed the expected difference, and when checked with a stricter method, only 2 held up.

The same paper combined 39 results from earlier studies (a meta-analysis). Overall, it found a weak link between media multitasking and distractibility (an effect size of 0.17). But after correcting for the tendency of small studies to show larger results, the link was no longer significant.

Why the results don't agree

  • Small samples: according to the 2018 review, most studies compared groups of 22 people or fewer, which makes results unstable
  • Cause and effect are unclear: current research cannot tell whether multitasking makes people more distractible, or whether more distractible people prefer to multitask. The review says studies that follow people over time, and larger studies, are needed
  • How it is measured: the amount of media multitasking is self-reported in a questionnaire

So we cannot say that media multitasking makes you less smart. What we can say is that some studies find heavy multitaskers have more trouble keeping their attention going.

Still, setting aside time to focus on just one thing while you study or work is something you can do without waiting for the research to settle. For practical ideas, see Can't stop picking up your phone at work? and How to stop reaching for your phone while studying.

FAQ

Does heavy media multitasking hurt your focus?

In the 2009 study, students who multitasked more were more easily distracted by irrelevant information. But many later studies found no difference, and in the 2017 replications only 2 of 14 tests showed a difference that held up. It is also still unknown whether multitasking is the cause.

Are heavy multitaskers better at multitasking?

In the 2009 study, they actually took longer to switch between tasks. In the 2018 review, heavy multitaskers did better in only 2 of 10 task-switching tests. Being used to multitasking does not mean you get better at switching.

Summary

  • In the 2009 study, students who media multitasked more were more easily distracted by irrelevant information
  • Later studies are mixed; in the 2017 replications, only 2 of 14 tests showed a difference that held up
  • The direction of cause and effect is unknown, and research continues
