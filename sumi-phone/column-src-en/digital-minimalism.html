You delete a few apps from your phone, and soon there are just as many again. You try a digital detox, and when it ends you slip back into old habits. If that sounds familiar, the idea of "digital minimalism" is worth knowing. It is not about cutting back through willpower. It is about choosing only what truly matters to you, and using just that. The idea was popularized by the American computer scientist Cal Newport.

The short version

  • Digital minimalism means using only a few tools that strongly support what you care about, and happily letting the rest go
  • Its originator, Cal Newport, published the book Digital Minimalism in 2019 (a Japanese edition is published by Hayakawa Publishing)
  • To start, take a break of about 30 days from the tools you can live without
  • After the 30 days, bring back only what you really need, with clear rules for how you will use it

What is digital minimalism?

Cal Newport is a computer scientist at Georgetown University, known for bestsellers such as Deep Work. In his 2019 book Digital Minimalism, he describes the idea roughly like this (from his blog post announcing the book):

Sharply reduce the time you spend staring at screens, focus on a small number of digital activities that strongly support the things you deeply value, and then happily ignore the rest.

The point is not cutting back for its own sake. It is deciding first what you want to use technology for, and keeping only what fits that purpose. Newport's concern is that we have taken up new technologies simply because they seemed handy, and our quality of life has suffered as a result.

How to start: take 30 days off, then choose again

In a 2016 blog post, Newport described the way he recommends getting started.

  1. Drop every tool you can live without: apart from anything your work or daily life truly depends on, take a short break from social media, video, games, news apps and the like. On the length, he says "30 days is about right, but the exact length doesn't matter much"
  2. Meanwhile, think about what you value: family, friends, health, learning, work. Write down what you think a good life looks like
  3. Bring back only what serves those things, with clear rules for use: decide the purpose and the method together, such as "I'll use this app, only at this time, to keep in touch with these people"

More than 1,600 people took part in an experiment Newport ran with his readers in January 2018. Participants reported that they missed the services they had dropped surprisingly little. Newport wrote that "the real problem, surprisingly," was "how to handle the sudden free time." That is why it helps to line up plans for time outdoors, or hands-on hobbies, before your 30 days begin (see 20 things to enjoy outside on your own).

Summary

  • Digital minimalism means using only a few tools that serve what matters to you, and letting the rest go
  • To start, take about 30 days off from the tools you can live without, then choose again
  • It lasts longer if you decide in advance how you will use the time you free up
  • Something to do today: write down your phone's apps in two lists, "could live without" and "would struggle without"
