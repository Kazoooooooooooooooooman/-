In 2025, the police in Japan recorded 27,832 cases of "special fraud" (tokushu sagi, Japan's term for scams that usually start with a phone call, such as fraudsters posing as relatives or officials), with losses of about 142.31 billion yen. Losses were almost double the year before (71.88 billion yen) and the highest in ten years. The big reason for the rise is "fake police" scams, in which callers claim to be police officers. This summary is based on final figures from the National Police Agency.

The short version

  • 27,832 cases of special fraud (up 32.3% from the year before), with losses of about 142.31 billion yen (up 98.0%)
  • Fake police scams: 11,014 cases and 100.50 billion yen, or 39.6% of all cases
  • Fake police scam cases were most common among people in their 30s (2,239) and 20s (1,718). This is not just a problem for older people

The trend in losses

Losses bottomed out in 2021 (28.20 billion yen) and have risen every year since, doubling in one jump in 2025. That works out to about 390 million yen a day. The average amount stolen per case was 5.236 million yen, and for fake police scams alone it reached 9.225 million yen.

What is a fake police scam?

The caller claims to be a police officer or similar official and says something like "Your bank account is being used for a crime" or "There is a warrant out for your arrest," then cheats the victim out of money in the name of an investigation. The National Police Agency began counting this method separately in 2025.

  • 11,014 cases and 100.50 billion yen in losses, about 70% of all special fraud losses
  • By number of cases, people in their 30s were the largest group (2,239), followed by people in their 20s (1,718)
  • By amount lost, people in their 70s (27.46 billion yen) and 60s (25.63 billion yen) lost the most
  • There are reports of scammers keeping victims on long video calls, or making them hand over gold bars, "to prove your innocence"

The real police will never ask you, by phone or video call, to transfer money or to show them your assets.

It starts with a phone call, especially from abroad

  • The police recorded 331,653 suspicious calls that probed for addresses, assets and similar details (so-called "warning sign" calls), up 71.4% from the year before
  • 75.5% of the phone numbers used in these crimes were international numbers (such as numbers starting with "+")

What you can do on the phone

  • On a landline, block international calls: apply to the International Call Blocking Reception Center (Kokusai Denwa Futoriatsukai Uketsuke Center) and you can block incoming and outgoing international calls on landlines and Hikari Denwa (fiber-optic home phone) lines for free
  • On a mobile phone, filter incoming calls: some apps can block incoming international calls. The National Police Agency has set up a system for recommending such apps
  • If a caller says they are the police, hang up first: then call back to check, using a police station number you looked up yourself or the police consultation line, #9110
