There are lots of apps meant to keep you from overusing your phone. Some set time limits, some grow a tree while you focus, some make you wait before opening an app. If you are not sure which ones work, research is the quickest guide. Based on an experiment published in 2023 in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), here is how effective apps work.

The short version

  • In an experiment with one sec, an app that makes you pause before opening other apps, app openings fell 57% in six weeks
  • What worked was a "back out" button and a short wait. Warning messages did not work
  • Your phone's built-in features (Screen Time / Digital Wellbeing) are enough to start with
  • If you tend to switch the limits off yourself, physically putting the phone away works better than any app

One pause before opening. That alone cut openings by 57%

Researchers at Heidelberg University in Germany and other institutions ran an experiment with an app called one sec. When you try to open an app you have chosen (such as Instagram), one sec shows a screen in between. That screen has a short prompt to make you think, a wait of a few seconds, and a button to back out of opening the app.

These are the results from 280 people who used it for six weeks, based on their actual usage data.

  • 36% of attempts to open an app ended with the app closed, unopened, after the interruption screen
  • Over the six weeks, the number of attempts to open apps itself fell 37% compared with week one
  • Taken together, the number of times apps were actually opened fell 57%

In a survey afterward, participants also said they spent less time on the apps and were more satisfied with how they used them.

What worked: the "back out" button and the wait

To find out which part made the difference, the researchers ran another experiment with 500 people (a preregistered online experiment).

  • Worked best: offering the option to back out of opening the app
  • Worked: making people wait a few seconds
  • Did not work: a prompt to make people think, such as "Do you really want to open this?"

In other words, people change their behavior more when you put a way out right in front of them and add a little friction than when you warn them with words.

If apps alone are not enough

Every app can, in the end, be switched off by you. If you tend to remove your own limits, these work better than adding more apps.

  • Switch your screen to black and white: Simply taking away the color weakens the urge to keep looking (how to set your screen to grayscale)
  • Physically put your phone away: Leave it in another room or put it in your bag. In the sense of adding friction, it works the same way as one sec

Summary

  • Making people pause before opening an app cut openings by 57% in six weeks
  • What works is an option to back out and a wait. Words of warning did not work
  • Start with your phone's built-in features. If you keep switching them off, put the phone physically out of reach
  • Something to do today: move the app you open most to the second page of your home screen or further back
