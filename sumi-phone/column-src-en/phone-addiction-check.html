If you searched because you thought "maybe I'm addicted to my phone" or "can it be diagnosed?", here's something to know first. Smartphone addiction is not an official medical diagnosis. What matters more than the number of hours is whether you can't stop on your own, and whether that is causing problems in your life. This article brings together an 8-question check you can answer right now, tests used by medical institutions, and places to get help.

The short version

  • "Smartphone addiction" is not an official medical diagnosis. For games, the WHO does recognize "gaming disorder" as a condition
  • The yardstick isn't "time" but three things: you can't stop, you put it ahead of other things, and you can't quit even when it causes problems
  • The check on this page is not a diagnosis. If you're concerned, try a medical institution's test or contact a support service

"Smartphone addiction" is not a diagnosis

Kurihama Medical and Addiction Center, which runs a specialist clinic for internet addiction, explains that "the medical term 'smartphone addiction' does not exist." What people usually call "smartphone addiction" is overusing the apps on a phone (social media, videos, games and so on).

For games, on the other hand, the WHO (World Health Organization) has included "gaming disorder" in the international classification of diseases (ICD-11), which has been in use since 2022. The WHO describes its features as the following three things continuing over time:

  1. You can't control how long or how often you play
  2. You put gaming ahead of other interests and daily activities
  3. You keep going even when bad things happen

On top of that, it has to be causing significant problems in your life. These three points are also a good yardstick when thinking about overuse of your phone as a whole. We could find no time-based standard, such as "more than X hours a day means addiction," in materials from medical institutions.

If you want to check in more detail

Kurihama Medical and Addiction Center publishes tests on its website for checking tendencies toward internet or smartphone addiction.

  • SAS-SV (Smartphone Addiction Scale – Short Version): A 10-question scale created by a Korean research group (Kwon et al., 2013). It has also been used in research on Japanese university students (Tateno et al., 2019)
  • IAT (Internet Addiction Test): A 20-question test created by Dr. Young in the United States. A note points out that because it's been around a long time, some questions don't fit how people use the internet today
  • DQ (Diagnostic Questionnaire): An 8-question test. It was used in a national survey by a Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare research group

You can take the tests from Kurihama Medical and Addiction Center's screening test page (in Japanese).

How many people struggle with this

In a 2024 MMD Labo survey of 10,000 people aged 18 to 69, a combined 62.5% felt they were "very dependent" or "somewhat dependent" on their phone.

According to estimates based on surveys by a Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare research group, the number of middle and high school students suspected of excessive (pathological) internet use rose from about 520,000 in 2012 to about 930,000 in 2017. (These are numbers of "suspected" cases judged by the survey questionnaire, not numbers of people diagnosed.)

You are not the only one struggling with this.

What you can do starting today

  1. Before bed, charge your phone outside the bedroom: That's the time when sleep is most easily cut short
  2. When you have things to do, put your phone in another room (how to stop picking up your phone while studying)
  3. Have a family member set the passcode for your limits (for people who keep switching off their own Screen Time limits)
  4. Try going without your phone for half a day (how to do a digital detox)

Where to get help

  • Mental Health and Welfare Centers: Located in every prefecture and designated city in Japan; you can also talk to them about overuse of the internet or games (nationwide list, in Japanese)
  • Kurihama Medical and Addiction Center, Internet Addiction Treatment Department: A specialist clinic opened in 2011. Check how to make an appointment in the appointment guide (in Japanese)
  • 24-Hour Children's SOS Dial (for children and parents): 0120-0-78310 (open every day, 24 hours, free; a Japanese helpline)
