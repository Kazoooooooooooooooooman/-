Forest bathing is studied around the world under its Japanese name, shinrin-yoku. Papers that pull many studies together mostly find that blood pressure and stress markers drop a little in forests. But each individual study tends to be small, and almost all of them measure only the changes right afterward. Here we separate what is known from what is not.

The short version

  • Many analyses find blood pressure, heart rate and salivary cortisol slightly lower in forests than in places such as city areas
  • Some analyses also find less anxiety and low mood
  • But most studies are small and short, and forest bathing cannot be called a way to treat or prevent disease

Blood pressure: a meta-analysis of 20 trials and 732 people (2017)

A research team from Gunma University and other institutions (Ideno et al., 2017) gathered studies comparing blood pressure in forests and in non-forest settings, and pooled their numbers (a meta-analysis). It covered 20 trials and 732 people: 17 trials were in Japan, 2 in South Korea and 1 in China.

  • Systolic blood pressure (the top number) was 3.15 mmHg lower on average in forests
  • Diastolic blood pressure (the bottom number) was 1.75 mmHg lower, and heart rate or pulse 3.84 beats per minute lower
  • The difference appeared both in trials where people walked and in trials where they did not (for example, sitting and looking at the view), so the researchers think it is not just a result of physical activity
  • The higher people's blood pressure at the start, the larger the difference tended to be

The paper is clear about its limits. Almost all trials measured only the change right after time in the forest, and only two lasted longer than a day. In one trial, the blood pressure difference had disappeared eight weeks after a three-day forest therapy program. The median trial had just 12 people, and the analysis did not take blood pressure medication into account.

Stress: salivary cortisol (2019)

An Italian research team (Antonelli et al., 2019) collected studies on cortisol, a hormone used as a marker of stress. They reviewed 22 studies and pooled the numbers from 8 of them.

  • In all but two studies, cortisol was lower in the forest groups, or fell after time in the forest
  • Salivary cortisol was lower in the forest groups than in the city groups
  • But the difference was already there before the forest visit. The researchers suggest that the expectation of going to the forest (a placebo effect) may play an important role, and call for more research because data are limited

When only randomized controlled trials were included, one analysis found no clear blood pressure difference. In other words, the conclusions about forest bathing can shift depending on which studies are pooled.

What to keep in mind

  • Many studies are small, and most were done in Japan, South Korea and China
  • Participants know they are going to a forest, so it is hard to rule out the effect of expectations
  • Most measure changes right after the visit; long-lasting effects are unknown
  • The 2026 analysis says forest bathing should be seen as a complement to health promotion, not as a way to prevent or treat disease

FAQ

Does forest bathing lower blood pressure?

A meta-analysis of 20 trials and 732 people (2017) found systolic blood pressure 3.15 mmHg lower on average in forests than in non-forest settings. But almost all of these were short-term changes, and a 2022 analysis of randomized controlled trials alone found no clear blood pressure difference. It is not a substitute for blood pressure treatment.

How long should you spend in the forest?

In the 2017 meta-analysis, most trials involved two hours or less in the forest. But none of the papers we reviewed established an ideal number of minutes.

Summary

  • Many analyses find blood pressure, heart rate and cortisol slightly lower in forests, along with less anxiety and low mood
  • Most studies are small and short, and one analysis rated the certainty of the evidence as "very low"
  • It seems best seen not as a treatment, but as one way to give body and mind a rest
