From August 1, 2026, Swedish law requires compulsory schools, the equivalent of primary and lower secondary school, to collect students' phones at the start of the school day and return them at the end. Until now, principals decided on this year by year; the law turns it into the same obligation nationwide. This summary is based on the Swedish government's bill, the Riksdag's decision and explanations from the Swedish National Agency for Education (Skolverket).

The short version

  • In compulsory schools and school-age childcare, phones are now collected in the morning and returned at the end of the day under a national rule
  • Exceptions are allowed, for example when a teacher instructs students to use them for learning, or for students who need support
  • Upper secondary school (gymnasium) is not covered

How the law came about

According to the bill, many compulsory schools (grundskola, equivalent to primary and lower secondary school) were already collecting phones for the whole day on the principal's decision before the law. In a survey of principals by the inquiry behind the bill, the share was about eight in ten. But principals had to renew that decision every year.

What does the law say?

The amendment is to Chapter 5, Section 4 of the Education Act (skollagen). It says students' phones are to be "collected at the start of the student's school day and returned at the end of the student's school day".

The preschool class is due to become the new first grade of compulsory school in 2028. Until June 30, 2028, a transitional provision applies the same rule to the preschool class.

When can phones be used?

According to Skolverket, the principal or a teacher can allow exceptions in cases like the following. Skolverket says exceptions should be applied restrictively.

  • When a teacher instructs students to use them for learning in class
  • When a phone is needed as an extra adaptation or special support for a student
  • When an individual student has other special reasons, outside teaching
  • During activities outside school, such as trips, when collecting and returning phones would be very impractical

Why a national rule?

The government's bill says phones can disrupt teaching and weaken concentration. It also points to concerns about bullying, such as insults online and offensive filming or photographing, to heavy phone use being linked to less sleep and less physical activity, and to students having less time with friends during breaks. The bill also notes that in Skolverket's 2024 survey, about half of compulsory school students felt they could generally concentrate in class.

At the same time, the bill acknowledges that research has not yet shown clear evidence of the benefits of collecting phones for the whole school day. Even so, it judged from the experience of the many schools already doing it that the measure works, and made it a national rule. Some bodies opposed it, such as the Swedish Association of Local Authorities and Regions (SKR), which said principals should keep deciding school by school.

FAQ

Are phones completely banned in Swedish schools?

In compulsory schools and school-age childcare, phones are collected at the start of the day and returned at the end. Exceptions are allowed, for example when a teacher instructs students to use them in class or for students who need support. Upper secondary schools are not covered by this rule.

When did it start?

The amended Education Act entered into force on August 1, 2026. The Riksdag decided on June 3, 2026. The government announced the start of the fall 2026 school year as a "phone-free school start".

Summary

  • From August 1, 2026, Sweden requires compulsory schools and school-age childcare by law to collect phones in the morning and return them at the end of the day
  • Before the law, about eight in ten compulsory schools were already collecting phones on the principal's decision
  • There are exceptions, such as use for learning or for students who need support, and upper secondary schools are not covered
