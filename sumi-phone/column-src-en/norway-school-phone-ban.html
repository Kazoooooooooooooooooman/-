Norway has no national law banning phones in schools. Instead, on February 7, 2024, the national Directorate for Education and Training (Udir) issued a recommendation: schools should strictly regulate phones through their school rules. Just over two years later, almost every school restricts phones in its rules. This summary of what the recommendation says and what school surveys show is based on material from the Directorate.

The short version

  • Norway's approach is a national "recommendation", not a law. The actual rules are set by municipalities and schools in their school rules
  • It is split by age: no phones in lessons or breaks in primary school; none in lessons and, as a rule, none at break in lower secondary; limits in lessons only in upper secondary
  • More schools now restrict phones, but staff say the effect is immediate yet does not solve everything, including bullying

What the recommendation says: three age groups

Because students get more say over their own lives as they grow older, the Directorate makes different recommendations for each stage of school. In Norway, primary school covers grades 1–7 and lower secondary covers grades 8–10.

What gets restricted is phone use that is not needed for learning. Upper secondary schools include vocational programs, so the Directorate asks that local rules also allow for cases where a teacher decides students need a phone for their training.

The recommendation has two goals: fewer disruptions in lessons so students learn more, and a better school environment with less bullying and harassment. The current version, updated on September 16, 2026, covers not only phones but other private digital devices too, such as smartwatches and AI glasses.

Why school rules and not a law?

The recommendation asks schools to set phone rules in their school rules (skoleregler). Under Norway's Education Act, school rules count as a local regulation. The Directorate recommends that the school owner, usually the municipality, set the rules rather than each school, so that every school in a town has the same age-appropriate rules.

  • When writing the rules, talk them through with principals, teachers, students and parents
  • Make room in the rules for students who need a phone at school because of health, social or learning needs (in both compulsory and upper secondary school)
  • If there are to be sanctions for breaking the rules, write those into the school rules too

Parliament also received a proposal for a national law banning phones in primary and lower secondary schools. In its report dated February 6, 2024, the Standing Committee on Education and Research recommended against adopting it. A majority of members preferred guidance that leaves room for local choices, and wanted to see how the recommendation worked in practice first.

What changed in schools: the survey numbers

Twice a year, the Directorate surveys a sample of principals and school owners across the country in a survey called "Spørsmål til Skole-Norge". In spring 2024 (March 20 to April 24), shortly after the recommendation, 96% of primary and lower secondary schools and 64% of upper secondary schools said their school rules restricted phones and smartwatches. By spring 2026 (February 24 to April 7), the share in upper secondary schools had also reached 96%.

How phones are put away depends on the stage of school. In the spring 2024 survey, 71% of lower secondary schools said students hand in their phones in the morning and get them back at the end of the day, using a shelf or box called a "mobile hotel" (mobilhotell). In primary schools, 61% said phones stay in the school bag all day. In upper secondary schools, the most common answer was that students hand in their phones at the start of each lesson and can use them at break.

Three in four principals in primary and lower secondary schools also said they already had similar restrictions before the recommendation. In upper secondary schools the figure was 36%. So the recommendation partly brought an existing trend into line nationwide rather than starting something new.

Effects and challenges

In 2026, NTNU Social Research (NTNU Samfunnsforskning), part of the Norwegian University of Science and Technology, published a report on bullying and school culture. It was based on two primary and two lower secondary schools, with workshops involving about 100 students and group interviews with 27 school leaders and staff.

  • Staff feel that a phone-free school gives students an important "breathing space" (pusterom), and that the effect was immediate
  • At the same time, bullying happens across settings, in games, on social media, after school and on the way to school, and gets carried into the school day. A phone-free school alone does not solve everything

Enforcement can be hard. In the spring 2024 survey, 67% of upper secondary principals and 35% of primary and lower secondary principals said enforcing the restrictions was challenging "to some extent" or "to a great extent". Some principals wrote that students hand in an old phone and keep another one.

Reading the numbers with care

  • The survey asks a sample of principals across the country. In spring 2024 the response rate was 37.9% for primary and lower secondary schools and 46.2% for upper secondary schools. The Directorate considers the sample representative enough
  • Spring 2024 asked about phones and smartwatches together, while the spring 2026 figure is for phones (37% of upper secondary schools also restrict smartwatches). The questions differ slightly, so treat the comparison as a rough guide
  • The figures show whether school rules restrict phones. They do not measure whether students actually stay off their phones
  • The NTNU report is a case study of four schools, not a measurement of the effect of phone-free schools in numbers

Elsewhere in the Nordic region, Sweden passed a law requiring schools to collect phones in the morning (Sweden's school phone law). Norway has gone the route of a recommendation and school rules. For the bigger picture, see school phone bans now cover 58% of the world's countries.

FAQ

Are phones banned by law in Norwegian schools?

No. As of November 16, 2026, there is no national law banning them. Municipalities and schools restrict phones in their school rules, based on the national recommendation the Directorate issued on February 7, 2024. In the spring 2024 survey, 96% of primary and lower secondary schools restricted phones in their rules.

Can students use phones in Norwegian upper secondary schools?

During lessons, the recommendation is to strictly regulate any use that is not needed for learning. For breaks, it does not recommend strict limits, given students' age. In the spring 2026 survey, 96% of upper secondary principals said their school rules restrict phones.

Summary

  • Norway restricts phones not by law but through a national recommendation from February 2024 and local school rules
  • Schools restricting phones in their rules: 96% of primary and lower secondary schools (spring 2024); upper secondary rose from 64% to 96% (spring 2024 to spring 2026)
  • Staff see an immediate effect, but say problems such as bullying are tied to life outside school too
