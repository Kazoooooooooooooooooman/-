You're stuck on a problem, you go out for a walk, and suddenly the answer comes to you. That experience is not your imagination. Experiments have confirmed both that walking increases your ideas and that walking in nature reduces brooding. Based on three studies and Japan's national guidelines, this article covers the benefits of walking and how to walk to get the most out of it.

The short version

  • Thinking while you walk increases the number of ideas you have. In an experiment, 81% of participants scored higher on a test of coming up with new uses for things
  • The effect lasts for a while even after you sit back down
  • People who walked for 90 minutes in nature brooded less than people who walked in the city
  • For your body, the national guideline is 60 minutes a day (about 8,000 steps)
  • To get the most out of it, walk without looking at your phone

Walking brings more ideas

In 2014, Oppezzo and Schwartz at Stanford University published four experiments. Participants took tests with no single right answer, such as "think of as many new uses for a brick as you can," once while sitting and once while walking.

  • People had more ideas while walking. In the first experiment, 81% of participants scored higher
  • The effect was the same whether they walked on an indoor treadmill or outside. It was the walking itself that worked, not the scenery
  • The effect lasted for a while even after they sat back down
  • However, on problems with a single correct answer (like riddles), walking did not raise scores

In other words, walking is better for widening your ideas than for finding the answer. Planning a project, working out how to structure a piece of writing, listing options for something you're worried about: at times like these, it is worth leaving your desk and going for a walk.

Walking in nature reduces brooding

In 2015, Bratman and colleagues, also at Stanford University, published an experiment in which participants walked for 90 minutes. Half walked in nature, among trees and plants, and the rest walked through a city with heavy traffic.

  • People who walked in nature were less inclined to brood over the same thoughts again and again (rumination)
  • Brain scans also showed lower activity in a part of the brain linked to rumination
  • People who walked in the city showed no such changes

So when you're feeling low, it helps to choose a green route, such as a nearby park or a path along a river.

For your body: 60 minutes a day (about 8,000 steps)

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "Physical Activity and Exercise Guide for Health Promotion 2023" recommends that adults walk, or do physical activity of similar intensity, for at least 60 minutes a day. In steps, that is about 8,000 or more a day.

The same guide also recommends moving even a little more than you do now. It is enough to treat 8,000 steps as something to aim for.

How to walk to get the most out of it

  1. Walk without looking at your phone: if you're looking at a screen, your mind has no room left to widen your ideas. Put your phone in your pocket, or leave it at home
  2. Pick just one thing to think about before you go: "next week's project," "the outline of my report," and so on. Don't try to reach an answer; just let your thoughts spread out
  3. Write things down as soon as you get back: the effect lasts for a while after you walk, so if you sit down at your desk right away, it is easier to turn your ideas into something concrete
