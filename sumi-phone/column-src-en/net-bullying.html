In fiscal 2024, schools in Japan identified 27,365 cases of bullying described as "being slandered or subjected to unpleasant things via computers, cell phones, etc." That is about three times the 8,788 cases in fiscal 2013, and the number has hit a new record almost every year. This summary is based on a survey by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT).

The short version

  • There were 27,365 cases of online bullying (24,678 the year before)
  • That is 3.6% of all bullying cases (769,022)
  • In high schools, 13.9% of bullying happens online. In elementary schools, 1.9%

Cases over time

The number has risen every year except fiscal 2014 (7,898 cases). MEXT says one reason bullying cases have increased is that schools have become more active in finding bullying online, such as on social media. So the rise reflects both more online bullying and better detection of it.

The share by type of school

How much of all bullying happens online varies a great deal by type of school (share of cases, with multiple answers allowed).

In high schools, roughly 1 in 7 bullying cases happens online. In elementary schools it is still rare, and bullying mostly takes the form of "teasing, mocking and name-calling" or "being lightly bumped into or hit." From junior high school, when more children have smartphones, the share of online bullying jumps.

Bullying overall

  • Elementary, junior high, high schools and special needs schools identified 769,022 cases of bullying, a record (732,568 the year before)
  • That is 61.3 cases per 1,000 students
  • There were 1,404 "serious incidents" caused by bullying, also a record

What families can do

  • Before giving a phone, agree that your child can tell you if something goes wrong: Bullying is most often discovered through school questionnaires (48.0%) and children reporting it themselves (19.6%). Being able to speak up is the quickest route to help
  • Make an escape from the group chat: Online bullying happens when relationships at school carry straight on inside the phone. If group messages keep arriving after your child gets home, there is no time to rest. Creating time offline, such as turning off notifications at night or putting the phone away at set times, is also a way to protect yourself
  • Keep evidence: Saving screenshots before messages are deleted helps when you talk to the school or the police
