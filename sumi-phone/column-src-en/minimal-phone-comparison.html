"I want to quit my smartphone and switch to a minimal phone." Start looking into it, and almost every model you find comes from abroad. In Japan, though, two things narrow your choices sharply: the radio law (giteki certification) and LINE (Japan's most-used messaging app). In this article, we compare minimal phones from abroad with phones you can buy in Japan in the same tables, and explain how to choose one you can use in Japan with confidence.

The short version

  • Among minimal phones from abroad, the Light Phone III is the only one we could confirm has Japanese giteki certification. We could not confirm giteki for the other models (as of October 2026)
  • None of the minimal phones from abroad is officially sold in Japan. Many of them cannot run LINE
  • The phones that are sure to work in Japan are the carriers' 4G feature phones, kids' phones, and small smartphones and flip-style smartphones sold in Japan

Before comparing: three conditions for using a phone in Japan

  1. Does it have giteki certification? In principle, any device that emits radio waves in Japan needs a "giteki mark," which shows it meets Japanese technical standards. MIC warns that using radio equipment without giteki may violate the Radio Act. Mobile phone signals (LTE and so on) are also not covered by the special system that lets you file a notification and try out uncertified devices
  2. Does it match Japanese networks? Separately from giteki, if a phone does not support the frequencies used by Docomo, au and SoftBank, it will struggle to get a signal. The difference shows especially in rural areas and inside buildings
  3. Can it run LINE? In Japan, there are still many situations where you need LINE for family or school communication. If you choose a phone that cannot run it, decide on another way to stay in touch (how to keep in touch without LINE)

Light Phone III

This is the third generation from the US company Light. It has only a fixed set of features, such as calls, SMS, camera, maps and music, and you cannot install apps. It obtained Japanese giteki certification on April 25, 2025, which you can confirm in MIC's registry (model TLP301, number 201-250244). There are also hands-on reviews reporting that it worked on Docomo, au and SoftBank networks. On the other hand, it is not officially sold in Japan, and import retailers sell it for around 130,000 to 180,000 yen. At launch, text input was English only (it can display Japanese). It is not yet suited to people who want to type SMS in Japanese.

Mudita Kompakt

It has a black-and-white e-paper screen and is based on Android with Google services removed. Its strengths are that it is easy on the eyes and the battery lasts a long time. The list price on the official overseas store is $459. We could not confirm Japanese giteki, and support for Japanese frequencies has not been published.

Punkt MC03

This is a privacy-focused smartphone from the Swiss company Punkt. Its screen is a large 6.67 inches, and it is closer to "a smartphone that protects your data" than "a phone with fewer features." In Japan, an earlier generation of its phones was once sold through a distributor, but we could not confirm Japanese sales or giteki for the MC03.

Minimal Phone

This model has an e-paper screen with a physical keyboard. It runs Android 14 with Google Play, so you may be able to install LINE and maps. That also means you can install social media. The product started on crowdfunding, and we could not confirm Japanese giteki.

4G feature phones are built for Japanese networks and are the safest choice. However, LINE does not work on some models, and some may stop working with LINE after LINE's changes in early November 2026 (can you still use a garakei in 2026?).

Flip-style smartphones and small smartphones are Android smartphones inside. They can run LINE and maps, but you can also install social media and video apps. If you choose one to cut down on smartphone use, you need to decide in advance what you will not install. Note that the BALMUDA Phone, which drew attention as a Japanese minimal phone, stopped being sold in September 2024.

Which one to choose

  • Calls and SMS are enough: a 4G feature phone. Cheap and reliable
  • You need maps, transit and LINE, but want to quit social media: a flip-style smartphone or small smartphone, used with a limited set of apps. But if you want to install something, you can
  • For an elementary school child: a kids' phone. From junior high up, if they need maps and messaging, choose a phone where parents can decide which features are available (at what age should kids get a smartphone?)
  • You want to choose by design and philosophy: the Light Phone III. It comes down to whether you can accept the Japanese input situation and the price
  • You want to keep your smartphone but use it less while you are out: keep your current smartphone as it is and get a second phone to carry when you go out (how to carry two phones)

Whichever you choose, check giteki with MIC's search before buying a model from abroad. Using an uncertified phone in Japan is not only a legal problem; it can also be inconvenient, with weak signal and, in some cases, missed earthquake early warnings.
