The "just think" experiments

A team from the University of Virginia and Harvard University in the US ran 11 studies. Most participants were college students. They handed in all their belongings, including cell phones and writing tools, and sat alone in a plain, undecorated room for 6 to 15 minutes. The only rules were to stay in their seat and stay awake. They were told to entertain themselves with their thoughts.

Across six studies using the same procedure, the results looked like this.

  • 57.5% said it was hard to concentrate (at or above the midpoint of the scale)
  • 89.0% said their mind wandered (same)
  • 49.3% rated their enjoyment at or below the midpoint of the scale

In a study done alone at home, 32% admitted to "cheating", for example by listening to music or checking their cell phones. There was no sign that people enjoyed it more at home. Compared with a group that did activities such as reading a book or listening to music, the "just thinking" group enjoyed themselves much less.

The people who chose a shock

In Study 10, participants first experienced an electric shock, and only those who said they would pay money to avoid being shocked again were included in the analysis. Then they spent 15 minutes alone with their thoughts. A button within reach let them give themselves a shock if they wanted.

67% of men (12 of 18) and 25% of women (6 of 24) pressed the button at least once. Men pressed it 0 to 4 times and women 0 to 9 times. One more man pressed it 190 times; he was left out of the average. The researchers think the gender gap is probably because men tend to be higher in sensation-seeking.

Note that this was a small group: 42 men and women in total. Even so, the researchers point to how striking it is that just 15 minutes alone with one's thoughts was unpleasant enough to make people choose a shock they had said they would pay to avoid.

The follow-up in 11 countries

To see whether the result held outside the US, a team that included some of the same researchers repeated the study with 2,557 participants at 12 sites in 11 countries (paper published in 2018). Participants were randomly assigned to "just think" or to "do something". In every country, "doing something" was more enjoyable.

There were differences between countries, but five individual traits explained them. People who enjoyed thinking more tended to have four things in common: they like to think, they are open to new experiences, they have meditation experience, and they started out in a good mood. On the other hand, people who reported using their phones more enjoyed the thinking time less. This shows a link only; it does not tell us whether phones are the cause.

Does quitting social media make you more bored?

For most of us, the handiest way to fill boredom is a phone. So what happens to boredom when people quit social media? Two studies came to different results.

  • A study by researchers in Germany and Austria (2018, 152 people): participants stopped using social media for 7 days and rated their mood three times a day. While they were off, boredom and the urge to use social media went up, and 59% slipped and used it at least once
  • A UK study (2023, 51 people): participants cut their social media use sharply for a week. Negative mood and boredom fell somewhat, but positive mood also went down

Neither study was large. Cutting back on social media may remove both bad experiences and good ones, so the overall effect may not be clear. For how to go about a digital detox, see how to do a digital detox.

FAQ

What was the electric shock experiment?

It was a 2014 study in the journal Science. During 15 minutes alone with their thoughts, participants could press a button to give themselves an electric shock. Among those who had said beforehand that they would pay to avoid a shock, 67% of men and 25% of women pressed it at least once.

Are people bad at being bored?

In this research, most people did not enjoy time spent doing nothing but thinking. A follow-up with 2,557 people in 11 countries found the same thing: doing something was more enjoyable. On the other hand, people who like to think or who had meditation experience enjoyed the thinking time more.

Summary

  • During 15 minutes alone with their thoughts, 67% of men and 25% of women gave themselves an electric shock
  • In a follow-up in 11 countries, doing something was also more enjoyable than just thinking
  • People who used their phones more tended to enjoy the thinking time less
